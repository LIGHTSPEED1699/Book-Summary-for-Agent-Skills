## Ch 1 — INTRODUCTION (file lines 264–350)

### 1.1 Historical Perspective (L270–288)
- Framing: over several decades, wide range of manual / semiautomated / fully automated approaches for fault diagnosis & prognosis (= "health management") on critical systems in **commercial and defense** markets; equally diverse philosophies and architectures per application.
- Earliest stage: early-generation aircraft relied on **manual detection and isolation on the ground**; systems analog, independent of one another; troubleshooting aids were only a schematic + voltmeter readings.
- As aircraft got more complicated/integrated: **built-in test (BIT) equipment, simple alarms, trending analysis** added to warn operators of safety-critical situations — but maintainers often still used voltmeter/schematics/operator reports; capability underused.
- OEMs/vehicle integrators then exposed fault detection alarm/BIT outputs to support troubleshooting: fault indications placed **on the front of a line-replaceable unit (LRU)** — originally mechanical indicators, later small **LEDs**; progressed through the **1950s–1960s**.
- LRU front panels often had a **test switch** commanding the LRU to self-test, mimicking ground-support-equipment (GSE) testing → capability named **built-in test equipment (BITE)**; began reducing need for GSE.
- Pitfall (stated): BITE of this era "often was confusing, not reliable, and difficult to use. Mechanics often distrusted it." LRU fault indicators could point mechanics in the right direction but schematics/voltmeters still widely used.
- Aircraft employing that practice: **Boeing 707, 727, early 737/747; McDonnell Douglas DC-8, DC-9, DC-10**.
- **1970s digital BITE**: complex systems began using computers for their calculations; fault detection/isolation info displayed in digital form, normally **numeric codes on the LRU front panel**; digital logic could better isolate cause; many different codes for each fault type; codes pointed to descriptions in a manual for isolation/correction.
- Aircraft with digital-BITE approach: **Boeing 757/767, Airbus A300/310, DC-10, Lockheed L-1011**.
- Pitfall (stated): separate front-panel displays became less effective as systems grew — each LRU used a **different display technique** for fault data; systems increasingly integrated with each other.
- **Digital data buses (ARINC 429)** emerged in this period; **autopilot systems among first** to use data buses and depend on sensor data from many other systems; their success drove definition of more sophisticated maintenance systems; sophisticated monitoring needed to meet **integrity and certification requirements of automatic landing function**.
- Example: **767 maintenance control and display panel** gathered maintenance functions of many related systems in one place.
- **ARINC 604 (defined 1986)** → **central fault display system (CFDS)**: one display for maintenance indications of all systems on the airplane; consistent access to maintenance data across systems; larger display than individual systems could host; **saved cost of front-panel displays on many LRUs**; CFDS selects the system whose maintenance data are wanted, routes that system's maintenance text to the display.
- CFDS applied to: later **Boeing 737s**, most systems on **Airbus A320/330/340**, **MD-11**.
- Pitfall (stated): as systems became more complex/integrated, **a single fault could cause fault indications across many systems**, even with CFDS; mechanic had little help distinguishing the **source fault indication vs. mere effect** indications.
- **ARINC 624 (early 1990s)**: more integrated maintenance system that can **consolidate fault indications from multiple systems** and provide additional functionality to support **condition-based maintenance**; minimal GSE needed since most test capability is inside the maintenance system; example: most factory functional tests on **Boeing 747-400 and 777** = executing selected tests + monitoring fault displays + monitoring certain bus data via the integrated maintenance system.
- **Evolution's goal (stated)**: identify the **single LRU or component that is the source of the fault**, so maintainer can remove the failed part with confidence.
- Pitfall/tradeoff (stated): sometimes isolation impossible **without adding sensors or wiring**; added sensors add components that can fail → can **worsen maintenance effort**, add **cost and weight**; benefits of improved fault isolation must be weighed against reduced reliability and added cost/weight.

### 1.2 Diagnostic and Prognostic System Requirements (L290–303)
- Core: effectiveness depends on ability to **detect faults early enough to do something useful** with the information.
- Chain: detection events **start** the process → fault **isolation and diagnosis** classifies the fault within the monitored system → **condition and/or failure prognosis forecasts the remaining useful life (RUL)**, defined here as "the operating time between detection and an unacceptable level of degradation."
- If the identified fault affects life of a critical component, **failure-prognosis models must reflect the diagnosis**.
- Requirements in **confidence and severity levels** must be identified for diagnosis and prognosis of critical failure modes; **detection level/accuracy should be specified separately from prognostic accuracy**.
- Minimum probabilistic specification for detection + diagnostic accuracy (2 items):
  - (1) **probability of anomaly detection**, including **false-alarm rate** and **real fault probability statistics**;
  - (2) **probability of specific fault diagnosis classifications** using **specific confidence bounds and severity predictions**.
- To specify prognostic accuracy, developer/end user must first define (3 items):
  - (1) the **level of condition degradation** beyond which operation is unsatisfactory/undesirable **to the mission at hand** (degradation threshold);
  - (2) a **minimum warning time** giving operator/maintainer actionable information **before** the failure/condition is encountered;
  - (3) a **minimum probability that RUL ≥ the minimum warning level** (i.e., P(RUL ≥ warning time) threshold).

### 1.3 Designing In Fault Diagnostic and Prognostic Systems (L305–309)
- Prognostic initiatives followed aircraft diagnostic evolution, motivated by **maintenance-planning and logistics benefits**.
- Pitfall (stated): early prognostic initiatives were **driven by in-field failures** (critical-safety or high-cost) → **retrofitted technology was hard to implement and costly to develop**.
- Consequence: developers needed to **analyze and describe benefits** of reducing in-field failures: positive impact on **safety, reliability, overall lifecycle-cost reduction** → many **cost-benefit analyses** and presentations to engineering management arguing diagnostics/prognostics must be **included in the design process, not an afterthought after field failures**.
- Payoff (stated): complex vehicle/system designs now developing **"designed-in" health management** inside an integrated maintenance & logistics system spanning the equipment's life: named programs — **Joint Strike Fighter (F-35), DD(X), Expeditionary Fighting Vehicle (EFV), various Future Combat System (FCS) vehicles**.
- The designed-in approach is performed **with the hardware design itself** and also serves as the **process for system validation**, for **managing inevitable changes from in-field experience**, and for **evaluating system design tradeoffs** (Fig. 1.1, "The designed-in approach to health management").
- Three realization ingredients (L309): (a) **synergistic deployment of component health monitoring technologies + integrated reasoning capabilities** for interpreting fault-detect outputs; (b) **learning technologies** to continuously improve the knowledge enabling the reasoning capabilities; (c) organizing elements into a **maintenance-and-logistics architecture** governing integration/interoperation within the system, between on-board elements and ground-based support, and between the health-management system and external maintenance/operations functions.

### 1.4 Diagnostic and Prognostic Functional Layers (L311–327)
- Comprehensive health-management philosophy **integrates results from monitoring sensors all the way through to reasoning software providing decision support for optimal use of maintenance resources**.
- Two core abilities of the strategy: (1) **accurately predict onset of impending faults/failures or RUL of critical components**; (2) **quickly and efficiently isolate root cause** once failure effects are observed.
- Payoffs: if predictions possible → **schedule replacement-parts allocation / refurbishment optimally**, reducing overall operational + maintenance **logistic footprints**; from isolation perspective → **maximize system availability, minimize downtime** via more efficient troubleshooting.
- Programs pursuing **autonomous logistics** concept need an **integrated maturation environment** for assessing/validating PHM system accuracy **at all levels of the system hierarchy**; inaccuracies quantified at each level then **assessed automatically up through the health-management architecture**.
- Accuracy-assessment method (Fig. 1.2, "Functional layers in the health management system architecture"): **quantify uncertainty at each individual level and accumulate inaccuracies as information propagates up the vehicle architecture** — final system-level reasoner/decision-support results are a direct result of propagated per-level results.
- Hierarchical **verification & validation (V&V)** / maturation process assesses technologies on: detect subsystem faults → diagnose root cause → predict RUL of faulty component → assess decision-support reasoner algorithms.
- Metrics named for this book: **accuracy, false-alarm rates, reliability, sensitivity, stability, economic cost/benefit, robustness**; technical performance/accuracy of algorithms = **performance metrics**; system-level capability vs. operational goals, economic cost/benefit, business-case assessment = **effectiveness measures** (detailed in **Chap. 7**).
- Implementation caveat: cost-effectiveness of a diagnostic/prognostic system **varies with design maturity and operational/logistics environment** of the monitored equipment.
- Common success element = **FEEDBACK loop**: when components/LRUs are removed from service, **disassembly inspections** must assess accuracy of diagnostic/prognostic decisions; system software and **warning/alarm limits optimized until desired accuracy and warning intervals** achieved; retain **selected degraded component examples for testing to define failure-progression intervals**.

### 2.1 Introduction (L359–383)
- **CBM definition (verbatim gist)**: use of **machinery run-time data** to determine machinery condition and hence current fault/failure condition, used to **schedule required repair and maintenance prior to breakdown**.
- **PHM definition**: refers specifically to the phase of **predicting future behavior, including remaining useful life (RUL), in terms of current operating state**, and scheduling required maintenance actions to maintain system health.
- Detecting a component fault/incipient failure for a **critical dynamic system (aircraft, gas turbine, pump, etc.)** and predicting RUL requires studies familiarizing the designer with the **physics of failure mechanisms** of the particular system/component.
- Designer must also master: **optimal selection of monitoring strategies, tools, algorithms** to detect, isolate, predict time evolution of faults; systems approaches for **design of experiments and testing protocols, performance metrics, V&V** of selected models. New systems approaches (and established ones from other disciplines) may be imported **with suitable modifications**.
- Methodologies reviewed in Ch 2: **trade studies** (formal framework comparing alternatives for selection of components, sensors, algorithms → "best" alternative technologies per specified requirements); **FMECA = the foundation for good CBM/PHM design**.
- FMECA works **in concert with reliability-centered maintenance (RCM)**; decides **severity, frequency of occurrence, testability** of candidate failure modes; per failure mode considers **fault symptoms + required sensor suite** to monitor behavioral patterns; advanced versions may list **candidate diagnostic/prognostic algorithms** best suited to identified failure modes.
- Data pitfall (stated): **new fault data usually required** for training/validating diagnostic and prognostic routines when historical data from on-system monitoring or testbench testing are **insufficient or nonexistent** → must devise and execute a **test plan** and assess **statistical significance** of collected data.
- **Technical and economic performance metrics** must be defined to guide design and evaluate the overall CBM/PHM system and its modules; performance assessment gets its own chapter (Ch 7) — here only a technoeconomic-analysis outline + the **V&V problem** introduced.
- **Fig. 2.1** = "An integrated approach to CBM/PHM design" — main modules with systems-based components highlighted; schematic shows **feedback loops** to optimize the approach and complete the data collection/analysis steps essential to developing fault diagnostic/prognostic algorithms.
- **Fig. 2.2 = "The CBM/PHM cycle": preliminary OFFLINE phase + ONLINE implementation phase**.
  - Online phase: machinery data from sensors → **signal preprocessing** → **feature extraction** (features most useful for current status/fault condition) → **fault detection and classification** → **prediction of fault evolution** → **scheduling of required maintenance** (detail in Chaps. 5 and 6).
  - Offline phase (background studies before online implementation): determine **which features matter most** for machine condition assessment; the **FMECA**; collection of **machine legacy fault data** to allow useful prediction of fault evolution; specification of **available personnel resources** to perform maintenance actions.

### 2.2 Trade Studies (L385–423)
- Scope: arrive at the **"best" or most balanced solution** to diagnosis/prognosis of critical component/subsystem failure modes → optimal CBM/PHM practices.
- Trade-study objectives (6 bullets): support system-engineering decision needs; **evaluate alternatives (requirements, functions, configurations)**; integrate and balance all considerations; recommend best solution; develop/refine a system concept; determine if **additional analysis/synthesis/tradeoff studies** are needed before selection.
- Formal framework = **integrated product and process design (IPPD)** (Schrage et al., 2002); IPPD steps: establish the need → define the problem → establish value objectives → generate feasible alternatives → make decisions.
- **DoD-mandated IPPD studies** focus on technology forecasting + **technology identification, evaluation, and selection (TIES)**; fault diagnosis/prognosis studies belong to the **TIES category**.
- IPPD work tasks: (1) define problem via **quality function deployment (QFD)** to capture customer requirements, define design tradeoffs, identify component functions/capabilities, monitoring & testing requirements; (2) establish value objectives + feasibility constraints; (3) generate feasible alternatives via **morphologic matrix** (technology forecasting/synthesis; identifies technologies for sensors, modeling tools; recommends selected system architecture); (4) evaluate alternatives with **Pugh concept selection matrix** against value objectives; (5) recommend decision from evaluation results.
- Tools & interactions (**Fig. 2.3**): **QFD / "house of quality"** (Sullivan, 1986) captures customer requirements, prioritizes them, lists component characteristics, ranks capabilities vs. state requirements — translates the **"voice of the customer"** into key "product and process characteristics/attributes"; **morphologic matrix (MM)** = technology-forecasting tool for initial synthesis of viable concepts, evaluates them qualitatively in **Pugh concept evaluation matrix (PCEM)** (Myers et al., 1989); weighted criteria (figures of merit) flow from QFD; MM identifies technology/subsystem solutions via brainstorming + engineering expertise, should include **"out of the box" and breakthrough technologies in the initial iteration**; first evaluation can be purely subjective (+/-), then **multiattribute decision making (MADM)** gives a more quantitative selection using weighted criteria.
- QFD mechanics: roof shows interrelationships between capabilities; ranking conventions **0–10 scale (10 most significant) or qualitative strong/medium/weak**; relative importance computed as **percent ranking**; CBM/PHM-relevant requirements: **size, cost, materials, durability, maintainability/replaceability**, etc.

### 2.3 Failure Modes and Effects Criticality Analysis (FMECA) (L425–552)
- Premise: **understanding the physics of failure mechanisms is the cornerstone of good CBM/PHM system design**; FMECA gives tools/procedures for a systematic, thorough design framework.
- FMECA essentially **relates failure events to their root causes**; addresses: identify failure modes + their **severity, frequency of occurrence, testability**; **fault symptoms** suggestive of system behavior under fault conditions; **sensors/monitoring apparatus** to track fault-symptomatic behaviors.
- Advanced FMECA may recommend **algorithms to extract optimal fault features/condition indicators, detect & isolate incipient failures, predict RUL** — "such studies generate the **template for diagnostic algorithms**".
- Deployment hook: FMECA framework may be **integrated into existing SCADA** or other data-management/control centers so operators get convenient access to failure events + root causes.
- Team composition required: **domain experts, reliability engineers, monitoring/instrumentation specialists, designers** of diagnostic/prognostic reasoners. Enabling tools ladder: **simple spreadsheet-type tables + explanation modules → rule-based expert systems, decision trees, Petri nets**.
- **Severity categories (4)**: 1 Catastrophic (death, significant injury, or total loss of equipment); 2 Critical (may cause severe injury, equipment damage, and termination); 3 Marginal (minor injury, equipment damage, or degradation of system performance); 4 Minor (no injury/damage but may result in equipment failure if left unattended, downtime, unscheduled maintenance/repair).
- **Frequency-of-occurrence classes (4): Likely / Probable / Occasional / Unlikely**, distinguished by **MTBF ranges**; example for MTBF = 10,000 hours: **Likely >1000; Probable 100–1000; Occasional 10–100; Unlikely <10** (note: OCR runs the labels onto separate lines; ranges attached as printed). Probability classification category **1–4 (possibly more), 4 = lowest probability**; classes separated on a **log power scale**; number derived from failure occurrence for the event standardized to a specific time period.
- **Testability**: whether symptoms/indicators of a failure mode **can be monitored via conventional or PHM sensors**; **excludes from the candidate failure-mode set those modes that cannot be measured**; may indicate additional sensing that would fix the problem, especially for **critical and frequent** modes; incorporated in FMECA **as comments**.
- **Example 2.1 — FMECA study for a Navy shipboard chiller** (ONR-sponsored, Propes et al. 2002): chillers support electronics/communications on navy ships (also commercial computing/communications, food-processing refrigeration); many failure modes (vibration-induced, electrical, process-related); most chillers already instrumented for vibration, temperature, fluid pressure, flow; Fig. 2.5 = U.S. Navy centrifugal chiller + its fault modes.
  - Sample fault record: Fault = **Refrigerant charge high**; Occurrence = **Probable**; Severity = **Critical**; Testability = **Can be monitored**; Description = due to **overcharge during maintenance**.
  - Physics: refrigerant stored in evaporator should barely cover cooler-tube tops at full load; high level covers tubes → less refrigerant boiled to compressors → **decreased efficiency, possible loss of cooling**; very high charge → compressor ingests **liquid droplets → rapid impeller erosion**.
  - Symptoms list: refrigerant level very high; increased full-load ΔT across chill water; low compressor discharge temperature; high compressor suction pressure; high compressor discharge pressure; high compressor motor amps; **or** compressor suction superheat < 0°F.
  - Comments (pitfall): optimal monitoring = a level gauge, but that may require impractical evaporator-shell modification; existing **site glass is a poor charge indicator** (load-condition discrepancies, tube/site-glass placement); refrigerant level should be monitored **only at normal full-load** (boiling slower at partial load → system holds more refrigerant at partial load).
  - Sensors list: level gauge/sensor; compressor suction pressure (10 inHg–20 psig); compressor discharge pressure (0–60 psig); compressor discharge temp (30–220°F); chilled-water outlet & inlet temp (20–60°F); **pseudo-compressor suction superheat sensor**; prerotation vane position; motor current sensing transformer.
- Failure-mechanism grounding example (motors, L453): **bearing failures = majority of motor failures** (improper/inadequate lubrication, shaft voltages, excessive loadings, vibrations); motor burnout from higher-than-normal current, overheating, load fluctuations; **inverter-fed motors** special modes: **pinhole insulation breakdown, reflected-wave overvoltages, heating from current harmonics, voltage buildup rotor↔stator via high-frequency switching** → "domain knowledge absolutely essential".
- **Example 2.2 — Pump system FMECA**: failure modes at seals, bearings, impeller & shaft, inlet strainer, valving; Fig. 2.6 = motor-pump arrangement with process + SCADA modules; frequency ranking variant here scaled 1–4 by event rate: **1 = <1 in two years; 2 = 1–3 every two years; 3 = 2–6 per year; 4 = >6 per year**.

### 2.4 System CBM Test-Plan Design (L554–591)
- Objective: design instrumentation, data-collection apparatus, testing procedures; run system **under controlled conditions in a test cell or under real operational regimes** to acquire **baseline + fault data to train and validate diagnostic/prognostic algorithms**.
- Critical concern: procedures to **seed fault modes safely** and acquire **fault progression data**; design of experiments guaranteeing a **statistically sufficient and complete database**.
- Systematic test-plan steps (6): determine system operation modes; decide a set of fault modes that **can be seeded safely** (decision based on related control variables and **Taguchi methods**); determine **sensor suite + data acquisition apparatus**; use **Taguchi matrix based on orthogonal array** for multilevel experiments; determine **number/type of test runs for baseline and fault data**, personnel requirements, budget; **"Make sure that sensors are calibrated!"** (emphasis as printed).
- Multilevel experiments: identify control variables (load, temperature, speed...), build **Taguchi matrix on orthogonal array** so dynamic range of control variables is covered per run (Montgomery, 1999); final run count dictated by **cost, time, personnel availability**.
- **TABLE 2.4 Taguchi matrix on L9 orthogonal array**: 4 control variables A/B/C/D each at settings **−1 lower / 0 normal / +1 upper**, 9 runs, result column 1=fault / 0=no fault.

### 2.5 Performance Assessment (L593–664)
- CBM/PHM serves **multiple end users**: the **maintainer** (determine "optimum" maintenance time under constraints: spare parts, personnel availability), the **operator / process manager / field commander** (confidence bounds on availability of critical processes/assets for production schedules or mission requirements), and the **system designer** (improve legacy-system operational capability; guide new designs to be more **fault tolerant, robust, reliable**).
- Performance-assessment studies evaluate **technical and economic feasibility**; technical performance metrics defined for **all algorithms from sensing & feature extraction through diagnosis & prognosis** (thorough treatment in Chap. 7 — "a major component in the design, evaluation, and implementation").
- Cost-benefit pitfall (stated, important): **sufficient performance data not yet available from installed systems to estimate true financial benefit / return on investment**; public studies rely on **"cost avoidance"** (estimated cost avoided vs. breakdown or scheduled maintenance).
- Cost-benefit procedure (6 steps): (1) establish baseline condition — if breakdown maintenance (BM) or time-based planned preventive maintenance is practiced, estimate cost & operational impact from maintenance logs (frequency, downtime, dollar cost) + qualitative impact via interviews with key personnel; (2) CBM reduces BM costs — **a good percentage of BM costs counts as CBM benefit**; (3) if time-based planned maintenance, estimate events avoided by **thresholding failure probabilities of critical components + computing approximate times to failure** — avoided-event cost = CBM benefit; (4) **intangible benefits**: impact of breakdown on operations assessed only by interviewing affected personnel + creating an **impact index (severe, moderate, ...)**; (5) roughly project **CBM cost**: instrumentation, computing, personnel training, installation & maintenance of monitoring/analysis equipment; (6) **aggregate life-cycle costs and benefits**.
- Closing method: technical and economic measures **weighted and aggregated in matrix form** to show management potential life-cycle benefits of the CBM system.

### 2.5.1 Verification and Validation (L666–687)
- V&V ensures delivered capabilities meet system design requirements; **system performance metrics are the foundation for V&V** and useful for evolutionary design; pitfall: **few formal or universally accepted techniques exist**.
- V&V supports **accreditation**; accreditation succeeds when the answer to "Do I trust that the system will meet the system performance specifications within stated constraints?" is affirmative.
- Definitions (as printed): **Verification = "Have I built the system right?"** (does the built system meet stated performance specs); **Validation = "Have I built the right system?"** (is the model close enough to the physical system; are specs & constraints correct).
- Hughes (1997) model-verification definition: "the process of determining that a model implementation accurately represents the developer's conceptual description and specifications."
- V&V framework module: **architecture understanding** — understanding a deployed CBM/PHM architecture is a major challenge due to inherent complexity, a significant hurdle to effective V&V. Approach: **decompose into a system of systems** with interaction constraints (partition into well-understood subproblems); component interactions captured in **OMG Interface Definition Language (IDL)**; system behaviors/architecture/component descriptions captured in **UML**.
- Offline architecture-understanding process (5 steps): build a set of CBM architectures (diagnostic, prognostic...) partitioned into functional modules; assign modules to a computational structure; choose **quality attributes/success criteria**; choose **concrete tasks testing those quality attributes**; evaluate degree each architecture supports each task.

### 2.5.2 Performance Metrics (L689–702)
- Metrics for diagnosis/prognosis functional elements = **core performance assessment of a deployed CBM/PHM system**; **simulation results + real fault data** project fielded performance; basis: sound **machinery condition monitoring + statistical signal-detection** techniques (full treatment Chap. 7).
- **Prognosis**: final output of the CBM/PHM system once a changing condition is detected and isolated; a **prognostic vector typically indicates time to failure + an associated confidence** in that prediction; metric definition must consider multiple viewpoints:
  - **Maintainer's viewpoint**: when to perform maintenance; most appropriate time given constraints (spares availability etc.).
  - **Field commander's viewpoint**: are assets available to perform the mission; confidence that critical assets remain available for mission duration; or given a confidence level, expected time to failure.
- Maintainer answer requires: estimate RUL + **assign uncertainty bounds to the trending curve** → earliest and latest (with increasing risk) maintenance times + **risk factor if action delayed**.
- Commander answer requires: confidence level in **mission completion** considering mission duration, expected operating/environmental conditions, contingencies; hence the **prognostic curve must be modulated by a time-dependent distribution (statistical/Weibull or possibilistic/fuzzy)** giving uncertainty bounds; an **appropriate integral of the distribution at each time** yields confidence of mission completion, or given desired confidence, the time to failure.
- Relevant V&V metrics: **accuracy and precision of the predicted time-to-failure probability density function** (details Chap. 7).

### 2.5.3 V&V Methods (L704–714)
- CBM/PHM = hardware + software + models (algorithms) working synergistically; system performance verified by **collecting actual/simulated data and asserting spec conformance**; model validation uses more formal methods.
- Formal verification core: ensure conditions implied by CBM/PHM code meet constraints from offline validation → **solve a Boolean expression that must evaluate to tautology**; satisfaction relation: **code/solutions must imply the specification** (every state the code allows must satisfy the spec's state description); control algorithms execute in an environment producing **symbolic expressions in terms of sensor readings** compared against specifications.
- **Model checking**: formal verification of **finite-state concurrent systems**; specs as **temporal logic formulas**; symbolic algorithms traverse the model; usable in either V or V process; if model is a finite-state machine the checker exercises **all permutations of each transition + interrelationships**; as a validation tool a conceptual model can be state-machined for **proof of concept**. Available checkers: **SVC, SMV, SPIN, Cadence** (Clarke and Wing, 1996); **SVC (Standard Validity Checker)** checks Boolean-expression tautology or produces a counterexample; pitfall/benefit: validating early in the cycle **significantly reduces later testing/debugging** → more reliable, easier-to-develop diagnosis systems.
- Validity checking needs the system specified in a **formal language (SMV, PROMELA)** + formal specs of desirable properties; checker determines properties hold **under all possible execution traces** — "exhaustively (but intelligently) considers the complete execution tree"; **SMV** (Carnegie-Mellon; Burch et al. 1994; McMillan 1993) among most popular symbolic model checkers; SMV = finite-type variables in a **hierarchy of module declarations** (local variables, initial values, state transitions); properties expressed in **CTL (computation tree logic)** — branching-time temporal logic reasoning over breadth and depth of the execution tree; SMV reports counterexample trace on inconsistency.
- V&V tooling: warehousing raw data, simulation models, system configuration + easy exercise of detection/isolation/prognosis algorithms adds value to acquisition programs; tool should incorporate **real data, simulation models, noise augmentation**. Concrete case: **Impact Technologies + Georgia Tech Web-based PHM V&V application for JSF (F-35) system suppliers** — standards & definitions, V&V metrics for detection/diagnosis/prognosis, **access to costly seeded-fault data sets** + example implementations, collaborative user forum, **automated impartial performance/effectiveness evaluation tool**; JSF supplier team being assembled: **Pratt & Whitney, Northrop Grumman, Honeywell**. System-accuracy approach = **quantify uncertainty at each individual level and build up to system level through the PHM architecture** (Fig. 2.8; mirrors Ch 1 Fig. 1.2).

## Ch 3 — SENSORS AND SENSING STRATEGIES (lines 998–1372)

### 3.1 Introduction (1004–1016)
- Sensors + sensing strategies are the foundational basis of diagnosis/prognosis. Strategic issues: sensor type, number, location; size/weight/cost/dynamic range; wired vs wireless.
- Raw transduced data rarely useful: must be preprocessed (filter, compress, correlate) to remove artifacts, cut noise, reduce volume — while preserving fault indicators needed to detect/isolate/predict.
- Sensor VALIDATION is explicit: "the sensors themselves are not subjected to fault conditions" — preprocessing must confirm data are "clean" before feature extraction.
- Feature extraction is "the most significant step" of the CBM/PHM architecture; extracted-feature vector is an essential input to diagnostic algorithms.
- Two sensor classes in monitoring: (1) classic/traditional transducers for process & performance variables (temp, pressure, speed, flow — dual-use for control + diagnosis); (2) "CBM/PHM sensors" placed exclusively to interrogate failure mechanisms: strain gauges, ultrasonic, proximity, acoustic emission, electrochemical fatigue sensors, interferometers.
- Open problems stated: few analytic techniques exist to optimize sensor placement/type/number; sensor fault detection/validation LAGS behind actuator/structure techniques; feature extraction "primarily an art relying on the designer's intuition".
- Feature doctrine (stated here, expanded Ch4): extract in time, frequency, wavelet, or chaotic domains depending on where dominant fault information resides; features should be UNCORRELATED, RANKED, NORMALIZED by contribution to the fault decision; final feature vector OPTIMAL w.r.t. discriminating ability for detect/isolate while meeting computational requirements and other constraints.

### 3.2.1 Transducing principles (1028–1040)
- Transducer = receives energy from one system, retransmits in different form; sensor = sensitive to light/temperature/impedance/radiation, transmits signal to measuring device. Two stages: (1) sense measurand (acceleration, pressure, strain, temperature), (2) transduce into signal suitable for transmission/conditioning/processing; transducer output usually electrical, then digitized (Fig. 3.1: measuring device schematic + piezoelectric accelerometer operation).
- Only a handful of physical principles underlie thousands of transducers (Allocca & Stuart 1984 taxonomy).
- Ideal: static (nondynamic) I/O — pure-gain transfer function when time constants small / bandwidth high; in practice dynamic effects non-negligible (size/material limits); electrical time constant typically an order of magnitude smaller than mechanical (de Silva 1989). Watch rise time, bandwidth, linearity, saturation when selecting.

### 3.2.2 Mechanical/structural sensors (1042–1130)
**Piezoelectric accelerometers (vibration):**
- Desirable properties (bulleted in text): wide usable frequency range; excellent linearity over wide dynamic range; electronically integrated signals give velocity & displacement; accuracy across environments; self-generating power; no moving parts → extreme durability; extremely compact, high sensitivity-to-mass ratio.
- Model (Fig. 3.2): piezoelectric element = stiffness k spring between base mass m_o and seismic mass m_s; force = seismic mass × acceleration → charge proportional to force; output ∝ base acceleration when seismic mass follows base over wide band. Displacement ratio R/R_o rises as forcing frequency approaches accelerometer mounted resonance [OCR: formula-not-decoded]. Usable range set by mounted resonance frequency; TRADE-OFF: high-frequency performance vs sensitivity. Materials: lead titanate, lead zirconate titanate (PZT), quartz.
- Examples named: B&K type 7704A-50 (Fig. 3.3); miniature triaxial DeltaTron types 4524 (Fig. 3.4).

**Strain gauges:**
- Principle: conductor resistance ∝ length & resistivity, /area; strain changes length/area → ΔR. Piezoresistive (semiconductor) gauges: ~two orders of magnitude higher sensitivity.
- Wheatstone bridge with 1, 2, or 4 active arms; output voltage indicates strain change.
- Named positioning example (Lackner 2004, wind-turbine-like blade): 4 gauges parallel to leading/trailing edges, ~1/3 span; two at 1/4 chord, two at 3/4 chord, mirrored across blade sides (Fig. 3.5); wired full bridge (Fig. 3.6): tension gauges at R1,R3, compression at R2,R4; V_EX excitation from amplifier; V_CH output.
- Full-bridge advantages (worked reasoning): CANCELS centrifugal stress (all four gauges same span → equal ΔR → V_CH zero under spin) and cancels thermal effects (uniform heating → equal ΔR) — a common-mode rejection argument worth reusing. Output → BNC → oscilloscope or DAQ.

**Ultrasonic sensor systems (contributed by J. & T. Michaels, Georgia Tech) (1098–1130):**
- Suited to structural health monitoring (SHM): waves travel long distances → monitor large material volumes; proven in NDI. Strategy: monitor CHANGES, then detect/localize/characterize damage — gives NDI-like sensitivity despite fixed sensors.
- Three wave types: guided, bulk, diffuse.
- Transducer hardware: piezoelectric disk in epoxy bonded to surface, nominal 2.25 MHz but broadband (effective <50 kHz to >3 MHz); commercial angle-beam transducers in U-shaped aluminum protection blocks, 10 MHz center, monitor very small cracks (Fig. 3.7).
- Guided waves: need interfaces to guide; Rayleigh/surface waves for surface/near-surface defects (penetration ≈ one wavelength); Lamb/plate waves for plate-like aircraft skins (metal & composite) but DISPERSIVE and hard to analyze. Detection/localization methods cited: Rayleigh time-domain differencing for crack size (Cook & Bertholet 2001); tomographic reconstruction of Lamb waves → damage images (Leonard et al. 2000; Gao et al. 2005); phased-array compact guided-wave arrays (Giurgiutiu & Bao 2004; Wilcox 2003); sparse arrays: image of drilled hole from only FOUR transducers (Michaels & Michaels 2006); differenced waveforms show scattering (Fig. 3.8).
- Bulk waves: longitudinal/shear in bulk material; for "hot spots" where damage location pre-known. Angle-beam shear-wave method for fastener-hole crack growth (Michaels et al. 2005; Mi et al. 2005): shear converts to creeping wave around hole; cracks block it; under load crack opening accentuates blockage → estimate CRACK AREA vs fatigue cycles (Figs. 3.9, 3.10).
- Diffuse waves: all modes equally excited; no identifiable echoes; "filled" structure. Acousto-ultrasonic (AU) NDI: record long, decaying diffuse fields. Pro: easy to generate, very sensitive to any structural change. Con: hard to discriminate damage from benign effects (environment, transducer coupling). Fixed transducers make SHM more tractable than NDI. KEY DISCRIMINATOR: local temporal coherence — similarity in shape of two signals vs time; damage → distinctive coherence loss vs baseline; environment changes preserve coherence through decay (Lu & Michaels 2005; Figs. 3.11 a–d progressive defect).
- Other: velocity/attenuation for gradual material deterioration; nonlinear ultrasonics promising for microdamage but very hard to implement in situ.
- Architecture (Fig. 3.12): combine ultrasonic methods synergistically; current+historical data fused with data-based AND physics-based methods → current structural state → prognosis.

### 3.2.3 Temperature sensors / thermography (1132–1180)
- Temperature = most-watched physical parameter after time; hot spots, large gradients, temps over spec = key incipient-failure indicators; monitor temp excess WITH other measurements for detect/isolate.
- RTDs: platinum wire/film, resistance vs temperature; Pt chosen for stability + near-linearity. Industrial Pt elements: −200 to 650°C; specialty to 850°C. Forms: (1) wire wound in ceramic (to 850°C), (2) wire encapsulated in glass / Pt film on ceramic substrate (highest vibration resistance, cheap mass production). Example: industrial Pt RTD series 21A straight probe, 195–482°C. Wired as one arm of Wheatstone bridge; LOW resistance ~100 Ω → four-wire connection to compensate lead resistance; excitation current causes SELF-HEATING — follow manufacturer limits & heat-path design; avoid mechanical strain. Pros: stability, repeatability, accuracy. Cons: cost (Pt), limited range (−200..650°C), slow response, damage by vibration/shock.
- Thermistors: thermally sensitive ceramic semiconductors; NTC typical −200..1000°C, PTC 60..180°C. Pick NTC for continuous R change over wide range; PTC for drastic change at a specific temp/current. Hermetically sealed: long-term stability, high R values, fast response, tiny sizes (0.005-in dia). Honeywell ISO-curve glass bead/probe (curve-matched interchangeability, matched pairs); PTC α ≈ 0.7%/°C typical, dissipation constants ~2.5 mW/°C. (Figs. 3.13–3.15.)
- Thermocouples: MOST widely used industrial sensor; rugged, subzero to >4000°F (2000°C). Seebeck effect: two dissimilar metals, junctions at different temps → emf ∝ temperature DIFFERENCE (hot vs cold junction — not absolute). Tables: mV vs °C with 0°C reference junction. Sensitivity 5–80 µV/°C. Wire pairs: iron-constantan (J), copper-constantan (T), chromel-alumel (K), chromel-constantan (E). Heavy-duty assemblies with metal-alloy protection tubes (NANMAC series A33, Fig. 3.16).
- Thermography for NDE: cracks generate heat under vibration (increased hysteresis → more thermal activity than undamaged region); thermal waves reach surface, pattern reveals defect when SYNCHRONIZED with excitation. Methods: thermoelastic stress analysis (TSA) and lock-in thermography (LT); demonstrated on subsurface cracks up to 20 mm thick (El-Hajjar & Haj-Ali 2004, Georgia Tech); infrared images of fatigue damage progression around rivet hole in 7050-T7451 specimen (Fig. 3.17) — rivet impedes transmitted heat but stress-concentration heat captured by TSA.
  - NOTE pitfall: "TSA" here = thermoelastic stress analysis, NOT time synchronous averaging (§4.3.4) — same acronym, different meaning.

### 3.2.4 Electrical measurements (1182–1194)
- Eddy-current proximity probes: mature technology for rotating-machinery protection; observe relative shaft motion inside bearing clearances (fluid-film interfaces) of high-speed turbomachinery. Monitor induced eddy-current response changes from material anomalies, cracks, shaft/mating-part displacement.
- Usage: detect lateral shaft motion, absolute/relative misalignment, bearing defects, cracks via XY ORBIT PLOTS from three strategically placed probes; compare amplitude/PSD/orbit plots unfaulted vs faulted (Figs. 3.18–3.19); crack detection in seal/rotor drive-shaft (Zou et al. 2000).
- MEMS: cheap, arrayable, batch-fabricated in silicon, integrable with electronics → on-board processing/"smart" functions; monitor temp, pressure, acceleration etc. Named parts: ADI three-axis iMEMS motion signal processing chip (tiny xyz accelerometer + conditioning on one low-power chip); Honeywell HG1900 (MEMS gyros + RBA500 accelerometers; low-voltage power; delta angle & delta velocity outputs).

### 3.2.5 Fiberoptic sensors (1204–1208)
- Advantages: compact/flexible, fabricable into arrays, batch fabrication; small, light, EMI/RFI immune, high/low temp endurance, fast response, high sensitivity, low cost. Measure strain, temperature, displacement, chemical concentration, acceleration.
- Technologies: extrinsic Fabry–Perot interferometry (EFPI), chemical change in cladding, optical signal change from fiber stress/deformation.
- Luna Innovations EFPI temperature sensors: tolerant/immune to high-T, high-EMI, corrosive environments; stable/accurate/linear over large range; tiny size → very small time response (Fig. 3.20). Vendors: Luna, KEYENCE, Techno-Sciences.

### 3.3 Sensor placement (1210–1234)
- Placement is an essential PHM/CBM design function; usually done by experience (number, measurements, physical access); guidelines exist for some military/aerospace systems (Kammer 1991); analytic work stems mainly from chemical industry (minimize sensor count: Alonso 2004, Chmielewski 2002).
- Sensor suite attributes: durability, robustness, accuracy, sensitivity over dynamic range of monitored variables; SENSOR USEFUL LIFE MUST FAR EXCEED monitored component lifespan (explicit requirement).
- Leverage existing control/performance sensors first; fault signatures may need additional PHM-specific sensors (accelerometers, proximity, acoustic emission, ultrasonic). Two placement levels: component level (e.g., bearing, 3-D object) vs SYSTEM level — fault propagates through multiple components; many candidate locations → hard combinatorial problem.
- Georgia Tech systems approach (Zhang 2005): framework = estimation + sensitivity analysis + OBSERVABILITY + optimization. Modules (Fig. 3.21): requirements analysis → system modeling → figure of merit (FOM) → optimization → performance assessment.
- Four placement requirements: detectability, identifiability, fault-detection reliability, limited-resource constraint; sensor uncertainty addressed explicitly.
- FAULT DETECTABILITY metric = collection (additive or multiplicative) of empirically defined terms, each explainable: SNR; TIME-TO-DETECTION / TIME-TO-FAILURE ratio (detection span from fault initiation vs failure span from initiation); fault detection SENSITIVITY = ratio of change in fault measurement to change in sensor measurement; symptom-duration / time-to-failure ratio. Uncertainty enters SNR & sensitivity estimates: compose probabilistic distributions of all noise sources, law of propagation of uncertainty. FMECA (Ch 2 §2.3) identifies fault modes/severity/frequency and maps candidate sensors to symptoms.
- Graph models for fault propagation: DG (directed graph, direction only; Raghuraj 1999), SDG (signed DG: +/− = same/opposite change direction; Bhushan & Rengaswamy 2000, Kramer & Palowitch 1987), QDG/QDM (QUANTIFIED directed graph: node = candidate sensor location with SNR in dB; branch carries sign + FAULT PROPAGATION TIME + PROPAGATION GAIN; Zhang 2005). Scan sensors on propagation paths → candidate types/locations.
- FOM = weighted sum of fault detectability and number of sensors; weights set by failure severity & probability of occurrence. Optimization = INTEGER NONLINEAR PROGRAMMING (commercial solvers; optimal or suboptimal). Pitfall: many faults × many locations → computational burden prohibitive → mitigate with PARTICLE-SWARM OPTIMIZATION (Eberhart & Kennedy 1995) heuristic per-fault sensor selection.
- Performance assessment estimates fault-detecting error rate: per-fault single false-positive & false-negative rates → averages.

### 3.4 Wireless sensor networks (1236–1343)
- WSNs where wires impractical (cost, weight, accessibility); fast install/calibration — UTA ARRI systems installed in machinery spaces in a FEW HOURS; breadth of disciplines required; architecture = data-acquisition network + data-distribution network + management center (Fig. 3.22).
- **Topology (3.4.1):** goal = prescribed message throughput + QoS (delay, due dates, bit error rate, packet loss, cost, transmit power). Node = compute + tx/rx over links. Topologies: fully connected, mesh, star, ring, tree, bus (Fig. 3.23); subnets of mixed topology; LAN vs WAN classes.
- **Power (3.4.2):** ad-hoc geographically distributed nodes (e.g., aircraft-dropped sensors) → extend node lifetime via power generation, conservation, management. MEMS rf transceiver components (caps, inductors; micro-inductors are the bottleneck). MEMS power generators: solar, vibration (electromagnetic & electrostatic), thermal (Figs. 3.24–3.25: dual vibrating coil, folded-beam suspension).
  - RF-ID: LC tank stores power from interrogation, replies; PASSIVE tags (no battery, limited storage) vs ACTIVE (battery, up to 1 Mb). LF 100 kHz–1.5 MHz; HF 900 MHz–2.4 GHz with range to 30 m. Applications: inventory, shipping, drive-by water-meter reading, toll collection.

### 4.3.1 Signal conditioning (1459–1487)
- Sensor signals: noisy, low amplitude, biased, dependent on secondary parameters (temperature); sometimes only a RELATED quantity measurable. Conditioning in hardware (VLSI, co-fabricated with MEMS) and/or software (Lewis 1992 reference).

- Analog active LPF (op-amp, Fig. 4.1): τ = R₂C; H(s) = ka/(s+a); 3-dB cutoff a = 1/τ rad; gain k = R₂/R₁. Rule: cutoff > highest useful sensor signal frequency.

### 4.3.2 Wavelet denoising / weak-signal extraction (1489–1497)
- Traditional denoising needs assumptions about signal vs noise separation; wavelet denoising needs only the signal CLASS FAMILY, not exact class.
- Principle: orthogonal wavelet transform compresses signal "energy" into a few LARGE coefficients; white-noise energy stays dispersed as small coefficients → thresholding removes noise while keeping signal.
- Soft-thresholding (Donoho 1995) and wavelet-shrinkage denoising (Zheng & Li 2000) = two popular methods [OCR: shrinkage formula itself sits in formula-not-decoded block; see §4.4.x if it reappears].
- KEY ADVANTAGE vs filter-based denoising: filters zero out-of-band components → kill impulse info spread over wide band; wavelet denoising only removes SMALL coefficients → impulses survive as LARGE coefficients.
- Applications listed: adaptive-threshold denoising in power systems (Yang & Liao 2001); acoustic-emission denoising for rotor-head fatigue cracks (Menon et al. 2000); knee-joint vibration SNR (Krishnan & Rangayyan 1999); fighter-aircraft structure fault detection by denoising residual signal (Hu et al. 2000); short-transient detection for localized roller-bearing defects via background-noise removal (Pineyro et al. 2000).

### 4.3.3 Vibration signal compression (1499–1511)

### 4.3.4 Time synchronous averaging, TSA (1513–1537) — gearbox/rotating machinery
- Needs pulse synchronized to rotation marking start of each revolution; ensemble-average numerous revolutions → one-revolution average. Enhances vibration components at MULTIPLES of shaft frequency (mostly gear-mesh after averaging out random vibration + external disturbance); shows per-tooth differences; ADVANCED local tooth damage detectable by direct inspection of time-averaged data (McFadden 1991).
- Problem: rotational speed wobbles → samples/revolution vary → INTERPOLATION to angle domain required (resampling freq as function of angular position); interpolation computationally demanding — bad for online real time.
- Alternative (Wu et al. 2005): take DFT of each raw revolution (NO interpolation), then ensemble-average complex numbers (amplitude AND phase) at common indices. Rationale: DFT resolution Δf = 1/T_shaft = f_shaft varies slightly per revolution; longer revolution → finer resolution + more indices, BUT mesh-frequency INDEX is invariant: e.g. planetary gear with 228 teeth always meshing at 228 × f_shaft, DFT index always 228 (and multiples). Average common indices, discard extra higher indices.
- Equivalence: averaging DFTs of raw data ≡ DFT of TSA data at common indices; difference only at spectrum-axis end; negligible at high sampling rates (e.g. 100 kHz) because revolution sample counts differ ~0.05% between revolutions. DFT linearity makes it exact for interpolated data.
- Angle-domain view: frequency in multiples of shaft frequency (not Hz) → invariant across speed variation; extracts/enhances invariant characteristics across revolutions (Figs. 4.3–4.6; TSA-in-frequency-domain code in book Appendix).

### 4.4.1 Feature selection & extraction doctrine (1549–1636)

### 4.4.2 Time-domain features / statistical moments (1638–1755)
- p-th moment via ensemble average over PDF f(x); if ERGODIC, ensemble ≈ time averages. p-th time moment over [1,N]; 1st moment = sample mean; 2nd = moment of inertia; ENERGY = sum of squares; RMS = sqrt(E/N).
- Moments about mean: 2nd = variance (biased sample estimate! → use unbiased /(N−1) version); 3rd = symmetry → SKEWNESS: zero for symmetric, positive = longer right tail, negative = longer left (Fig. 4.11 two PDFs same mean/std, different skew).
- KURTOSIS = 4th standardized moment: measures contribution of TAILS; higher-kurtosis PDF = more peaked center + fatter tails (Fig. 4.12); distributions can share mean/variance/skew yet differ in kurtosis. Kurtosis "especially useful in fault diagnosis (Engel et al. 2000) because it captures the bang-bang types of motion owing to loose or broken components."
- Correlation/covariance: autocorrelation R_x(0) = energy; autocovariance P_x(0) = σ²; cross-correlation/cross-covariance for two series; Pearson correlation; convolution = polynomial multiplication; correlation expressible via convolution (time-reversal).
- MATLAB toolkit named: moment, kurtosis, skewness, mean, std, var; cov, xcorr (Signal Processing Toolbox), conv.
- Moving averages/time windows: properties change with time → window of size N sliding at current time n (keeps newest N samples, discards old); moving average MA(n,N) — smaller N → MA responds faster; moving variance; windowed versions of other moments analogous.

### 4.4.4 Wavelet-domain processing (1901–1999)
- When each applies: FFT fine for stationary, NOT suitable for nonstationary. Time-frequency options: STFT — constant resolution at all frequencies, cannot give good time AND frequency resolution simultaneously, no orthogonal fast STFT basis; Wigner-Ville — good both resolutions but SEVERE interference (cross) terms; wavelets — multiscale via dilation α + translation β; high freq → good time/poor frequency resolution, low freq → poor time/good frequency resolution (Fig. 4.24 scalogram usage). Morlet concept 1984.
- TABLE 4.1 comparison (as bullets): CWT — good freq/low time res for low-freq components, low freq/high time res for high-freq; no interference; FAST. STFT — depends on window, either good time or good freq; no interference; slower than CWT. WVD — good time & freq; severe interference; slower than STFT. Choi-Williams (CWD) — good both; less interference than WVD; VERY slow. Cone-shaped (CSD) — good both; less than CWD; very slow.
- Wavelet basis ψ_{α,β}(t); W_ψ(α,β) inner product with conjugate; |W| modulus = energy vs time+frequency — physical interpretation used for fault signals.
- SCALGRAM = square of CWT modulus (threshold θ applied); coefficients → fault-feature vectors to train classifiers; coefficient selection: statistics-based criteria (Yen & Lin 1999, 2000), wavelet packets for reduced set, PCA to shrink feature space (Akbaryan & Boshnoi 2001 [OCR: name garbled]).
- Energy-based features: wavelets' key edge = IMAGE VISUALIZATION of signal energy → compare signals, spot anomalies; feature = energy per scale/band directly [formulas OCR?]. NAMED EXAMPLE: helicopter planetary gear (Saxena et al. 2005) — energy distribution among the FIVE planets became ASYMMETRIC when a crack appeared on the gear plate → feature = increasing VARIANCE among the five planets' energy values over time. Lesson: design a feature that turns a visible scalogram property into a number.
- Coefficient-based features: compact support → energy concentration; most c_mn tiny, discardable; thresholding picks survivors as feature set. MORE SUITABLE FOR EARLY FAULTS than energy-based (slight changes → too-small energy changes).
- Singularity-based features: from wavelet phase maps — detect discontinuities/impulses; singularity exponents from envelope of vibration diagnosed breaker faults (Xu et al. 2001); shaft center orbits (Peng et al. 2002b). Modulus maxima method → Hölder exponents as fault features (Mallat & Hwang 1992); reveals anomalies AND sometimes fault LOCATION (power systems, Dong et al. 1997); also electrohydraulic servo faults (Chen & Lu 1997), reciprocating compressor valve vibration (Lin et al. 1997), rub-fault position in rotating machines (Zhang et al. 2001). PITFALLS: singularity methods NOT robust to noise → denoise first; wavelet must be sufficiently REGULAR (long filter impulse response) to detect all singularities.
- Application domains listed: gearboxes (Chen & Wang 2002; Essawy 1997; Hambaba & Huff 2000; Zheng 2002; Yen & Lin 2000); rolling-element bearings (Altmann & Mathew 2001; Shibata 2000); analog circuits (Aminian & Aminian 2001); system ID: wavelets to extract impulse response & identify modes/mode shapes/damping (Robertson, Freudinger et al.), viscous damping + natural freq of hydrogenerator axle (He & Zi 2001; Ruzzene 1997), bearing dynamic characteristics (Ma et al. 2000). Impulse-response methods expensive + noise-sensitive; frequency-response methods suffer energy leakage + spectrum overlap — wavelets' time-frequency + compact support + filtering overcome these.

### 4.5.1 General vibration-analysis method set, SKF field practice (2005–2038)
- FFT SPECTRUM ANALYSIS: certain machinery problems occur at certain frequencies → look for amplitude changes in certain frequency bands.
- ENVELOPE DETECTION (high-frequency demodulation, the bearing one): filter out high-frequency ROTATIONAL vibration, ENHANCE repetitive components at bearing DEFECT frequency range; most common in rolling-element bearing + gear mesh analysis where low-amplitude repetitive defect signal is hidden by rotational/structural noise; outer-race defect: each roller rolls over the defect each revolution → small impact at bearing defect frequencies. (This is the "when enveloping applies" rule: hidden repetitive impulses under high-frequency carrier noise.)
- SPECTRAL EMITTED ENERGY (SEE): acoustic-emission-based VERY EARLY bearing/gear-mesh detection; AE caused by: bearing defects, contaminated lubrication, lack of lubrication, dynamic overloading, microsliding/fretting, bearing friction, cavitation/flow, electrically generated signals, metal cutting, compressor rotor contact, electrical arcing, cracking/tin cry. Sensor "listens" for ultrasonic emissions in the 150–500 kHz range ("not so much vibration as high-frequency sound"). BEST tool for earliest bearing problems (subsurface/microscopic defects that cause NO measurable vibration yet). Gradual SEE rise ≠ immediate replacement — enough prewarning for operational/lubrication corrections to save/extend bearing; sharp rise → shorten measurement intervals, multiparameter monitoring, TREND SEE-enveloping + temperature + vibration together to time corrective action.
- PHASE MEASUREMENT: a measurement not a processing method — angular difference between shaft reference mark and vibration signal → amplitude context, shaft orbit, shaft position; key for balancing.
- HIGH-FREQUENCY DETECTION (HFD): numerical overall value of high-frequency vibration in 5–60 kHz band pass from small internal flaws; sensor resonant frequency inside band pass amplifies low-level flaw-impact signal; measured with accelerometer, displayed IN G's; peak or RMS overall.

### 4.5.2 Engine blade crack time-of-arrival case study (2040–2076)

  3. SIDEBAND LEVEL FACTOR (SLF): sum of first-order sideband amplitudes about fundamental dominant mesh / std dev of time signal average (Keller 2003) [formula OCR?].
  4. ENERGY RATIO (ER): std dev of DIFFERENCE signal / std dev of REGULAR signal; regular signal = inverse FT of all RMCs; difference signal = time signal minus RMCs [formulas OCR?].
  5. ZERO-ORDER FIGURE OF MERIT (FM0): peak-to-peak of time-averaged signal / sum of amplitudes of regular meshing frequencies over whole spectrum [formula OCR?].
  6. VARIANCE OF ENERGY RATIO (VER): describes per-revolution inconsistency because crack at post 4 makes plate ASYMMETRIC → energy ratio computed per segment: split regular + difference signals into 100 segments, ER per segment, VER = std dev of the 100 ER values (GAG 1→1000 as crack grew from 1.4 in).

### 4.6 Real-time image features & defect classification (2190–2236)
- Machine-vision inspection scheme (Fig. 4.49): color/gray image → PREPROCESS + SEGMENT to find suspected defect targets → extract features per target → CLASSIFY; repeat segmentation+classification until conclusion at prespecified confidence. Online near-real-time required for incipient-failure imaging (hot spots, corrosion precursors); same tools work offline. Georgia Tech applied (Ding et al. 2002).
- Preprocessing: enhancement needed for noise + clutter; ACTIVE CONTOURS ("snakes") segmentation via level-set theory (Yezzi et al. 2000): region-based curve-evolution equations + statistics — natural for BIMODAL images; driving forces depend on region STATISTICS not absolute thresholded intensities → robust to intensity variations across image; FULLY GLOBAL (each contour point sees all image data); downsample + good initial contour estimate to speed up. Demo: poultry fan-bone inspection online in a processing plant (Fig. 4.50, snake evolution n = 150, 850); works when bone contrasts well with tissue.
- Feature families: GLOBAL (position + contrast vs neighbors); COLOR (absolute RGB, HIS/HSI intensities + relative relationships); SHAPE — boundary-based: radius mean+std, curvature, perimeter, Fourier descriptors; region-based: aspect ratio, area, moments. Selection metrics: separability, robustness to segmentation inaccuracy, robustness to color variation.
- Classification example: regions = fan bone / shadow / edge (dark regions from bone, dark meat, meat→background transitions); part claimed bone-free ONLY when all its regions classified non-bone.
- RECURSIVE segmentation+classification (the confidence-feedback trick): snakes fail (undersegmentation) when defect touches a similarly dark transition region → merged binary region distorts shape → misclassification. Use CLASSIFICATION CONFIDENCE as segmentation-quality measure: accurate segmentation → feature vectors separable → high confidence; under/over-segmentation distorts shapes + intensity distribution → vector drifts from cluster → low confidence → RE-SEGMENT (e.g., reapply snake to undersegmented clip treating it as bimodal with new initial contour) until confident (Figs. 4.51–4.52).

### 4.7 Virtual sensors (2238–2264)
- Premise: no "fault meter" exists — e.g., no sensor directly measures bearing crack dimension in operation; direct sensors absent, too expensive, or location inaccessible; yet the state is crucial. Virtual sensor (= soft/analytical sensor) = available sensors + DSP/decision mapping to infer quantity of interest. Kalman filter cited as a typical virtual sensor (detailed later in book = Ch 5).
- WORKED EXAMPLE (orifice): flow Q nonlinear in pressure drop: Q = K√(P1 − P2) [OCR: formula rendered as text; standard orifice law, K constant] — treat as LOCAL model relating Q and ΔP; if ΔP measured AND VALIDATED but flowmeter impractical, Q is an ANALYTIC MEASUREMENT inferred from the model.
- Diagnostic implication: map known measurements to a "fault measurement"; realize as NN or any mapping construct. Two pitfalls: (1) NN needs lab/constructed experiments for training; (2) the physical underlying measurement (orifice ΔP) MUST be validated first.
- Sensor-data VALIDATION via PARITY RELATIONS: residual between measurements of same variable from different sources; validated estimate built from CONSISTENT SUBSET of inputs (Fig. 4.54); parity-space technique: use inconsistency to identify failed inputs and exclude them. RULE OF THREE: three measurements needed to decide if a sensor is faulty; voting scheme picks the faulty one.
- NN virtual sensor named example (Marko et al. 1996): recurrent NN classifier for engine MISFIRE detection — inputs: crankshaft acceleration, engine speed, engine load, engine ID; output = misfire diagnostic evaluation. Same concept → virtual sensor mapping measurable features → TIME EVOLUTION of fault pattern. Implementation options: neural network, look-up table, fuzzy-logic expert system, or other mapping tools (Fig. 4.55).

### 4.8 Fusion / integration technologies (2266–2368)
- Three fusion levels (Fig. 4.56): (1) DATA level: combine multisensor raw data to validate signals + create features — example: speed signal + vibration signal → time-synchronous-averaged vibration features. (2) FEATURE level: combine features intelligently — example: oil particle count/size feature fused with vibration KURTOSIS → improved bearing-health confidence. (3) KNOWLEDGE level: incorporate experience info (legacy failure rates, physical-model predictions) with signal-based info.
- CENTRAL PITFALL: fused system can perform WORSE than the best individual tool — poor estimates drag down good ones. Fix: a priori WEIGHT tools by capability/performance; degree of a priori knowledge = f(system understanding + operating history); choose the fusion method based on a-priori information characteristics.
- Architectures: CENTRALIZED (fuse raw data; align+correlate first; theoretically most accurate; impractical data load for large real-time sensor networks; Hall & Llinas 1997). AUTONOMOUS (feature extraction BEFORE fusion → dimensionality reduced; less accurate than raw fusion because raw signal info discarded). HYBRID (fuse both raw + features, fusion center can "tap" raw data; often most practical) (Figs. 4.57–4.59).
- Technique menu with rules of thumb:
  - BAYESIAN: P(fault|observed output) from P(output|fault)·P(fault) [formula OCR?]; plain Bayes handles only discrete classifier outputs → modified version fuses a priori failure prob at time t [P_FO(t)], classifier output prob [C_D(i,t)], time-independent feature reliability [R_D(i)]; guard against division by zero [formulas OCR?]. Cons: a-priori distributions may be unavailable; INSTABILITIES with conflicting data or many unknown propositions.
  - DEMPSHER-SHAFER: explicitly tracks lack-of-information (fixes Bayesian a-priori problem). Construction as described here: each input feature has FUZZY MEMBERSHIP FUNCTIONS giving POSSIBILITY of a fault mode; each feature = an "expert"; possibility values → BASIC PROBABILITY ASSIGNMENTS (mass functions) per feature; DEMSTER'S RULE OF COMBINATION assimilates evidence masses → degree of certainty per fault mode (Shafer 1976; generalization of probability, strong on ambiguous data + ignorance). [OCR: combination rule + conflict normalization K formulas are NOT printed in this chapter's OCR — the formal D-S concepts are deferred to Ch 5 §5.4.3 (pointer at line 2322). Pitfall noted here: DS can become IMPRACTICAL for time-critical large fusion problems.]
  - WEIGHTING/VOTING: weights from a priori accuracy knowledge; ONLY constraint: weights sum to 1; fused confidence = Σ W_i·C_i over features [formula OCR?]; simple but WRONG WEIGHTS → fused output worse than best individual (same pitfall as above).
  - FUZZY-LOGIC INFERENCE: scale membership functions per rule base; combine via summation / maximum / "single best"; defuzzify fused output via centroid, max height, or midpoint. Example (Fig. 4.60): image features (tonal mean, midtones, kurtosis, others) → single output ranking probability of anomalous/foreign object present.
  - NEURAL-NETWORK FUSION: real-time combination + optional autonomous relearning; unsupervised learning discovers data properties / groups similar patterns (good for large training sets); supervised uses a "teacher" specifying target outputs.
- Worked fusion experiment (run-to-failure industrial gearbox): torque cycled 100→300% load starting ~93 h; drive gear multiple broken teeth; test stopped ~114 h. 26 vibration features from ONE housing accelerometer (simple RMS → residual-signal measures from TSA waveform after mesh+sideband removal; Byington & Kozlowski 1997). Kurtosis + NA4 shown: NA4 = quasinormalized kurtosis of residual r = 4th moment of residual / (run-time average variance)²; run-time avg variance = average residual variance over run ensemble up to current point; NA4 nondimensional, nominal 3 for purely Gaussian residual (Figs. 4.61–4.62). Ground truth = borescopic inspections of pinion + drive gear bounding no-damage → single-tooth-failure window, + which features best detect tooth cracking pre-failure → a-priori for fusion.
  - Parameter settings: best 7 features weighted 0.9, average 0.7, low 0.5 (voting); same numbers = feature reliabilities in Bayesian fusion; DS uncertainty values: best 0.05, average 0.10, low 0.15; prior failure probability from experience: drive-gear crack forms at MEAN TIME 108 h, VARIANCE 2 h.
  - Outcome: all three (DS, Bayesian, voting) rise at ~108 h (index 269). VOTING most susceptible to false alarms; BAYESIAN rises early but can't reach high confidence; DEMSTER-SHAFER early detection + higher confidence + more sensitivity through the failure transition region (Fig. 4.63, all 26 features). Fusing only the BEST 7 → more accurate and stable; DS retains high confidence with enhanced robustness in the critical transition (Fig. 4.64). Backprop NN trained supervised on 4 top features (RMS, kurtosis, NA4, M8A) using ground-truth probability-of-failure curve as target; NN auto-adjusts weights/thresholds; residual gap vs ground truth = post-optimization error; once trained, reusable for different tests under similar conditions (Fig. 4.65).

### 4.9 Usage-pattern tracking (2372–2382)
- CBM/PHM must track USAGE not just sensor data: aircraft regime recognition (maneuvers + automated gross-weight calc) needed to estimate remaining life of life-limited components; actual usage severity vs analytic model can EXTEND published life limits; maneuver identification must match load-survey criteria; gross weight (GW) + weight distribution (CG) hardest usage variables to measure automatically; schemes to deduce GW from flight parameters exist.
- Loading-profile concept: load spectrum profiles as inputs to fatigue damage models for RUL prognosis; expressed via ground-air-ground (GAG) cycles, cycle time, or operational time; archived usage → probabilistic damage accumulation from system's "birth" to now = INITIAL DAMAGE DISTRIBUTION for prognosis after fault detection/isolation.
- Named result: plasma-etch reactor (Rietman & Beacky 1998): 3.5 years of continuous usage streams, monitored gas flow-rate STD DEVIATION fluctuations → NN predicted a pressure failure 1 HOUR before it happened. Later (Rietman et al. 2001): 23 processing steps / 200 tools monitored simultaneously, independent + combined NNs, auto-generated Pareto charts to yield engineers (proactive defect detection in manufacturing).
