# Chapter 4: Linear Controllers

## Core Idea
Choosing a controller is choosing a *criterion* first, then a mode set, then settings — in that order. Shinskey's criterion is minimum IAE (integrated absolute error), because it guarantees damping while keeping IE nearly optimal, and because IE is the economic quantity (IE = I·Δm/P, so every dollar of deviation maps to proportional band and integral time). The chapter then shows the three liabilities of the classic modes — derivative gain limits, integral windup, and PID mode interaction — and the two escapes from the PID frame: model-based control for dead-time-dominant processes, and sampling control, which is the same idea without needing a model match.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Error magnitude (shutdown risk)**: for a step load, the deviation cannot be affected until one dead time has passed. Best possible for a non-self-regulating capacity **e_b = Δq·τd/τ₁** (4.2); for a self-regulating one **e_b = Δq·Kp(1 − e^(−τd/τ₁))** (4.3). A properly tuned PI controller peaks at **1.48 e_b** and PID at **1.13 e_b** (non-self-regulating); for τ₁ ≈ τd, 1.08 e_b and 1.02 e_b. **So no tuning can beat e_b by more than ~48%; the rest must come from feedforward.**
- **IE = ∫e dt = I·Δm/P** (4.4) — integrated error from the control algorithm itself. Consequences: (a) if the manipulated variable returns to a previous value, IE over that interval is zero (useful for batch cycles); (b) for IE to be zero the controller must integrate and work linearly — nonlinear compensation may be applied to the controller's *output*, never its *input*; (c) minimize both P and I, but not at the cost of damping; (d) derivative does not appear in the formula, but typically lets I be cut 2–3×, and so cuts IE by the same factor.
- **Minimum IAE as the general criterion**: minimum IE permits a sustained cycle (IE = 0 while IAE grows without bound). Loops tuned for minimum IAE give IE only ~10% above the minimum (Table 4.1), i.e., ~5% of the load-response area falls on the far side of set point.
- **Statistical criterion**: σ is a transformation of deviation over many samples. Set point may be positioned relative to a specification limit: at 3σ the limit is violated 0.15% of the time. Subgroup size must match the process's ability to absorb variation (a 24-h storage tank shrugs off minute-by-minute variation; bottling at 3000 units/min does not), and averaging must mirror the vessel's mixing — a perfectly mixed tank is a first-order lag and calls for the incremental model x_t = x_(t−Δt)·(1 − Δt/τ) + x_(t−Δt)... i.e., weight each new sample by Δt/τ (4.7), not equal weighting.
- **Set-point vs load response**:
  - Dead-time process: proportional loop gain < 0.5 means no proportional overshoot on a set-point step, so I alone sets overshoot. Optimum load settings also suit set-point steps. Best possible **IAE_b = |Δr|·τd** (4.8); the best PI is 39% worse (P = 266Kp, I = 0.47τd, IAE = 1.39 IAE_b). P-on-c alone: P = 220Kp, I = 0.405τd → 1.66 IAE_b; I-only: I = 1.6τd → 2.05 IAE_b.
  - Integrating (non-self-regulating) process: a set-point change needs no integral action at all, and providing it guarantees overshoot — the output must merely move temporarily and return. Best possible **IAE_b = |Δr|(τd + 0.5 t_r)** (4.11), with ramp time t_r = Δr·τ₁/[100(1 − q/100)] upward (4.9) and the mirror form downward (4.10) — so up and down ramps only match at 50% load. PD, P on deviation, D on c: P = 120τd/τ₁, D = 0.4τd → 1.49 IAE_b; P and D both on deviation: P = 140τd/τ₁, D = 0.42τd → 1.24 IAE_b. Setting **D > I** (P = 360τd/τ₁, I = 0.55τd, D = 1.9τd) also suppresses overshoot.
  - One technique to kill proportional response to set point: pass the set point through a first-order lag exactly equal to I. Another: go to manual while the set point is stepped, then transfer back — the bumpless initialization then acts on integral alone.
- **Cyclic load sensitivity γ(τq)**: the loop's gain and phase at each load period (4.15). For a non-self-regulating process under PI (I = 4τd, from Table 1.2), sensitivity peaks near **6 dead times** (γ ≈ 3.34) — near the loop's own damped period (5.45τd) — and rolls off on both sides; at long periods, integral control provides the attenuation, at short periods, capacity and proportional action. Practical rule: **keep the peak γ in the range 2–3** for acceptable damping. A 1:4 decay ratio corresponds to γ_max = 2.0 for dead time under P control, 2.22 under integral control. This is also the mechanism by which an oscillation in one unit propagates down a series of identical process units.
- **Mode interaction (interacting vs noninteracting PID)**: noninteracting sums P, I, D vectors (4.24–4.26); an interacting controller applies I and D serially, whose effective settings are **I_eff = I + D** (4.29) and **D_eff = 1/(1/I + 1/D)** (4.30), with steady-state gain **Kc = (100/P)(1 + D/I)** (4.31). Three consequences: D_eff can never exceed ¼ of I_eff (reached at D = I); when D > I, D moves I_eff more than D_eff, and I moves D_eff more than I_eff; around D ≈ I, a change to either shifts all three modes by about the same percentage. Where integral feedback is taken from the *output* but derivative is still in the output path, gain becomes **Kc = (100/P)(1 + D/I)/(1 − D/I)** (4.33) — infinite at D = I and *negative* beyond it, so D must stay well below I.

## Key Concepts
- **Derivative gain limiting** (10–20 typical, by limited-gain amplifier or a first-order lag of time constant D/K_D). Table 4.3: with a limit of 10 the effect is negligible for τ0 > D; at τ0/D = 5 (normal PID operation) gain is unchanged and phase lead drops only 7°; at τ0/D = 1 the ideal 81° lead becomes 48.8°. Raising the limit to 20 buys back 3.6°, lowering it to 5 costs another 7°.
- **Integral windup**: positive feedback through lag I (Fig. 4.4) drives m to saturation while e ≠ 0; windup is complete when the lag output b is also saturated, and then **control cannot resume until the error reverses** — so windup always produces overshoot. Narrowing the output limits only helps if they are not inside the manipulated-variable range. The fix is a **batch unit** in the feedback path with a **preload** m_q (Fig. 4.5) — preload must be set *below* the anticipated steady-state load, and integral time may need to be longer than the load-regulation optimum.
- **Bumpless auto/manual transfer** works the same way: in manual, the automatic output tracks the manual regulator through the integrating feedback (with I bypassed for speed), so on transfer to automatic the output integrates from where it was, with no proportional kick. Some controllers offer set-point initialization instead — the choice depends on whether the goal is to hold position or to resume at the previous set point.
- **Optimum PID settings for step load changes (Table 4.4, P in units of τd/τ₁)**:

| τd/τ₁ | Interacting: I/τd, D/τd, P, τ0/τd, IAE/IAE_b | Noninteracting: same columns |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 0.77, 0.35, 72, 2.8, 1.25 | 0.92, 0.32, 55, 2.3, 1.13 |
| 1.0 | 1.03, 0.40, 88, 2.9, 1.58 | 1.17, 0.37, 67, 2.6, 1.44 |
| 0.5 | 1.17, 0.48, 105, 3.0, 1.70 | 1.43, 0.41, 74, 2.7, 1.61 |
| 0.2 | 1.43, 0.52, 105, 3.0, 1.86 | 1.77, 0.41, 76, 2.8, 1.79 |
| 0 (NSR) | 1.57, 0.58, 108, 3.1, 1.99 | 1.90, 0.48, 78, 2.9, 1.94 |

The big win over PI is on the non-self-regulating process: IAE/IAE_b falls from **4.58 to 1.99**. Noninteraction adds under 3%.
- **Robustness is the price of tight tuning**: as loop gain *increases*, the loop cycles near its natural period as expected — but as loop gain *decreases*, damping barely improves while the damped period can more than double. For a PID tuned to minimum IAE on a non-self-regulating process, doubling P moves the sensitivity peak from ~3 to ~10 dead times with a higher peak; tripling P moves it to ~13τd. Diagnosis: **two integrators in the loop** (process + controller) make controller phase approach 90° at long periods. Check τ0 against I and D: for a PI on a non-self-regulating process, **I should be 0.75 of the damped period**; for PID, **I ≈ 0.5 and D ≈ 0.18 of the damped period**. If they are lower, the controller contributes too much lag: raise I and D, or reduce P.
- **Process identification** (needed before any table applies): open-loop — step the output, draw the maximum-slope line, read τd at the baseline intersection, τ₁/Kp from the time to traverse Δm; Kp = Δc/Δm for a self-regulating process, and for non-self-regulating use the bottom row of the tables. Closed-loop — maximize I, minimize D, narrow P until the loop cycles (pulse it manually if the cycle will not start); an undamped cycle gives τn and Pu directly. For non-self-regulating or τ₁ ≫ τd processes, τn = 4τd and **Pu = 63.7·Kp·τd/τ₁** (1.52).
- **Closed-loop settings are more forgiving than open-loop ones** (Table 4.6): ratios I/τn and D/τn vary less across τd/τ₁ than I/τd and D/τd do, so assuming the wrong τd/τ₁ costs much less. Checked response targets: PI optimum damped period ≈ 1.35 τn regardless of τd/τ₁; interacting PID ≈ 3τd for all ratios.
- **A second capacity**: for τ₂ up to ~0.2(τ₂+τd) it may be treated as extra dead time (the open-loop estimate of τd actually measures τ₂+τd, which is fine for D but **overestimates P** if τ₂ > 0.2τd). Once τ₂ dominates, it sets the period while dynamic gain barely moves — so P stays a function of τd/τ₁ (108τd/τ₁ for small τ₂, 120–130 for larger). As τ₂ grows relative to τd, optimum I and D converge; once they are equal in an interacting controller, no further benefit is available from crossing them and a noninteracting controller is required.

## Model-based control (the dead-time answer)
- **Principle** (Fig. 4.13): the controller contains a model of the process; a signal characterized to match the process response is fed back *positively* to cancel the negative feedback from the process, so the output stays at its instantaneous correct value and oscillation disappears. Matched conditions: **P = 100Kp and gc = gp** (4.35).
- **Dead-beat response** — IAE = IAE_b — is achievable on a pure dead-time process, and critically **at a proportional loop gain of 1.0** (versus P = 235Kp for PI), because the two feedback paths cancel. Loop gain sets the character (Fig. 4.16): 1.0 = dead-beat, 1.5 = quarter-amplitude decay, 2.0 = uniform oscillation at 2τd.
- **When it wins**: matched model IAE = Kp(τd + τ₁)·Δu (4.37); it beats PI above a fractional dead time of **0.65** and PID above **0.75**. For higher fractions it is the controller of choice, and it is also a viable PID substitute where noise forbids derivative.
- **Robustness is the cost**: a process of pure dead time — the one that benefits most — is the least tolerant; with no process capacity any dynamic mismatch destabilizes. A process time constant as small as 0.1τd is enough to give stability for controller dead times between 0.5τd and 1.1τd, and stability improves as τ₁ grows. Robustness can be bought with a first-order filter on the controlled variable (matched by a lag in the feedback model), which adds IAE exactly as process capacity does. Non-self-regulating processes can also develop offset, because the model-based controller cannot integrate.
- **Tuning**: set controller dead time = process dead time, D = half of the smaller of τ₁ and τd, and the controller time constant ≈ D/2; P from Table 4.8 (100Kp for pure dead time, 90Kp at τd/τ₁ = 5, down to 60τd/τ₁ at 0.2). Dynamic matrix control takes its model directly from a sampled open-loop step response, so it matches processes more complex than dead time + first-order lag, but the reference claiming superiority over PID for single-lag processes was comparing against badly tuned PID.

## Interrupting the loop (sampling)
- **Sampling adds an effective dead time of half the sample interval**: φ = −180°·(Δt/τ0)·... in Shinskey's terms the average age of the information acted on is Δt/2 (4.39). A loop whose only dynamic element is the sampler cycles at **2Δt** under proportional control; under continuous integral control the period becomes **2Δt + 4τd**.
- **PID on sampled measurements is problematic**: the stepwise variable makes derivative fire pulses of amplitude (step size × proportional gain × derivative gain limit). Two remedies: pass c through two first-order lags each of Δt/2 (Ryskamp), or use a sampling algorithm.
- **Sampling controller (the recommended answer)**: enable the controller for a short control interval Δtc at the start of each sample interval, then hold it in manual. Dead-beat response on a sampled dead-time process then needs integral action only, with **I = Kp·Δtc** (4.43); loop gain < 1 is exponential, 1–2 gives damped oscillation at 2Δt, 2 gives uniform oscillation. It is **recommended over model-based control, even on continuous measurements**, because it only requires the sample interval to exceed the dead time rather than a faithful model match — at the cost of not seeing a disturbance until the next interval. On an electronic controller, drive the auto/manual switch with a repeat-cycle timer (off time ≳ dead time, on time much shorter); for a noisy signal the control interval must be long enough to integrate a representative value. Settings: continuous PID values apply, with I and D multiplied by Δt/Δtc; a non-self-regulating process needs the dead-time term in all three settings augmented by Δt/2.

## Digital control
- **Emulating analog is a downgrade**: phase lag from sampling, derivative gain limits, and resolution all cost performance. The advantage is algorithm latitude — blocks for input scaling/linearization, square root, multiply/divide, summation, dead time, lead-lag, PID, curve characterizer, selector, logic, timers, ramp — plus on-line restructuring and adaptive features.
- **Positional algorithms** (preferred, external feedback): derivative filter **y = y + Δt(c − y)/(Δt + D/K_D)** (4.45), then proportional **with limits** (4.46), then **b = b + Δt(f − b)/(Δt + I)** (4.47) for integral. The incremental ("velocity") form (4.48) loses reliable proportional action, cascade anti-windup, and clean selective control, because it carries no fixed bias.
- **Sample-interval selection is a two-sided constraint**: too long adds phase lag; too short invites offset from finite resolution (with Δt = 1 s, I = 60 min and 15-bit precision, the smallest effective deviation is 11%). Derivative gain is set by D/Δt, which should stay in the 10–20 range, implying **Δt ≈ 0.01τn**; the effective derivative gain is Δt/D + 1/K_D (4.49), so K_D must be computed against D/Δt (4.50), capped around 50, or the intended limit of 10 delivers only 5.
- **Limited resolution**: a 12-bit converter is 0.025% of range, fine for flow and analyzers. On a 400°F temperature span each count is 0.1°F; with P = 10% and derivative gain 10, **one count steps the output 2.5%** — which is why derivative is discouraged in many digital temperature loops. Threshold-keyed ("report-on-change") transmission inside a loop is worse: the steady state cannot be reached exactly, and the resulting limit cycle has an amplitude at least as large as the threshold.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging a controller by a single disturbance type** — settings optimal for step load are not optimal for set-point steps (unless the process is pure dead time) nor for pulses (which need no integral action at all).
- **Tuning to minimum IE alone** — a sustained cycle has zero IE.
- **Applying nonlinear compensation inside the controller's input** — IE stops being zero.
- **Chasing a long period by widening or narrowing one mode** — you cannot tell a gain reduction from a dead-time increase without a test; use the I/τ0 and D/τ0 diagnostics.
- **Assuming a sampling controller works "because the loop is digital"** — the algorithm must be keyed to the sample event; running a continuous PID on a chromatograph output gives derivative pulses and worse performance than the sampling form.
- **Reporting "digital is better"** — a digital controller emulating analog is measurably worse.

## Worked Example
**Best achievable vs tuned PID on the same non-self-regulating process**: with τd/τ₁ = 0.5, PI needs I/τd = 1.17 and P = 105Kp (Table 1.2) for IAE/IAE_b = 1.70, and IAE exceeds IE by ~10% consistently. Switching to interacting PID (Table 4.4) gives I = 0.67τd, D = 0.48τd, P = 105Kp, period 3.0τd and IAE/IAE_b = 1.70 → 1.70 for PI vs ... the headline case is the pure non-self-regulating process (τd/τ₁ → 0): PI 4.58 versus PID **1.99**, with no control action felt until two dead times have passed and derivative turning the variable back in about one-third the time. That ratio — not the last few percent from a smarter mode — is where the design effort belongs.
