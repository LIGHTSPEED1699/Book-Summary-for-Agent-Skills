# Chapter 8: Interaction and Decoupling

## Core Idea
Every real unit needs at least two loops (rate and quality), so pairing valves to controllers is a decision — and with no basis, a designer has a 50:50 chance of getting two loops right and 1 in 6 for three. Bristol's **relative gain** settles it: compare the open-loop gain with all other loops open against the same gain with all other loops closed. Unity means no interaction; anything else measures exactly how much the other loops will fight this one. The direction of the deviation from unity is the design rule that matters — **gains between 0 and 1 cost period and require detuning; gains above 1 do not cost period but make set-point response stiff; negative gains invert the response and are only conditionally stable** — and when interaction is intolerable, a decoupler cancels it, at the price of an error tolerance that shrinks as the relative gain grows.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Relative gain**: λ_ij = (∂c_i/∂m_j) with all other m constant ÷ (∂c_i/∂m_j) with all other c constant (8.1). Zero means c doesn't respond to m with others fixed; infinity means the other loops prevent the response entirely (a non-independence, i.e. a pairing that cannot work); 0.5 is the worst interacting-but-stable case in a 2×2.
- **RGA properties**: rows and columns sum to 1.0, so a 2×2 needs only λ₁₁ and a 3×3 needs four entries. If any element exceeds 1, a negative element must exist in the same row *and* column. All elements between 0 and 1, or all outside, in a 2×2.
- **Scale-free and nonlinearity-free**: λ is dimensionless, so it is insensitive to ranges and scaling, and a nonlinearity appears in numerator and denominator and cancels — which means a known functional relation (y = f(x), or y = f(x,F) where F is not manipulated) can be substituted directly, and the process model can be linearised before differentiating.
- **Expand the variable list before pairing**: manipulated variables need not be valve flows. A ratio (A/B) or a sum/difference (A+B, A−B) is available whenever a secondary controller could manipulate it — as the composition controller does with a ratio, and a level controller with a flow difference. For a 2×2 that turns two elementary variables into four, giving six combinations and **12 possible configurations**; for 3×3, nine useful variables give 432 viable configurations against just 6 elementary ones — so there is almost certainly a better configuration than the obvious one. First choices: temperature and composition respond to flow **ratios**; level and pressure respond to **sums and differences**.
- **Calculation methods**:
  - *Ratio method*: differentiate the process model, keeping the result in terms of the variables themselves (Ex. 8.1, two valves in series: λ₁₁ = (p₀−p₁)/(p₀−p₂), λ_h2 = (p₁−p₂)/(p₀−p₂) — the valve with the greater pressure drop is the better flow-control valve, and if p₁ is midway all elements are 0.5 so the pairing does not matter).
  - *All loops open*: c₁ = K₁₁m₁ + K₁₂m₂ etc.; substituting for the other manipulated variable gives **λ₁₁ = 1/[1 − K₁₂K₂₁/(K₁₁K₂₂)]** (8.11) — and the sign count decides the range: an **odd** number of positive K terms puts λ between 0 and 1, an **even** number puts it outside. Parallel-stream flow loops (Fig. 8.3) have an even count, giving λ₁₁ = 1/[1 − (K₂₁/K₁₁)²] (8.12), i.e. typically 1 to 2, infinity if the cross-gains match.
  - *All loops closed*: write m = H·c; H is the inverse of K, H_ji = 1/(∂c_i/∂m_j) (8.15), and **λ_ij = K_ij·H_ji** (8.16). **λ_ij = 1/[1 − H₁₂H₂₁/(H₁₁H₂₂)]** (8.17).
  - *Slope method* (2×2, best for columns): perturb one manipulated variable and record the slope dc₁/dc₂; repeat for the other; **λ₁₁ = 1/[1 − (dc₁/dc₂)_m1/(dc₁/dc₂)_m2]**. Used for distillation compositions against reflux, reflux ratio, product flow.
  - *Matrix method*: for higher order, invert K (or H) and multiply element-wise by the transpose; the chapter gives the 3×3 determinant, four inverse elements, and four relative gains, the rest by difference (Ex. 8.2 fills a 3×3 RGA from nine open-loop gains).
  - **Integrating elements need not be modelled**: a level loop can be treated as a flow loop for RGA purposes because the integrator appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels.
  - *Incrementing is not differentiating*: if increments are used, keep them small and average both directions.
- **Decomposing big systems**: where some loops are much faster (level, pressure vs composition) they can be assumed closed, reducing a 5×5 to a set of 2×2 subsets — for distillation, 10 subsets from 5 manipulated variables, more once ratios are included. When comparing subsets, the choice is always between λ values on opposite sides of unity, so you must know how the two regimes behave.

## Effects of interaction
- **The general response with controller 2 in automatic**: dc₁/dm₁ = K₁₁g₁₁ − K₂₁g₂₁K₁₂g₁₂/(K₂₂g₂₂ + 1/(K_c2g_c2)) (8.34). If the other controller's gain is very high at this loop's period (a fast flow loop beside a slow composition loop), the term vanishes and dc₁/dm₁ ≈ K₁₁g₁₁(1 − λ₁₁) — so the slow controller's band must be **readjusted by 1/λ₁₁** once the flow loop is closed. λ near 1.0 costs nothing; λ near zero costs enormously and signals wrong pairing. A *dynamic* relative gain replaces each steady-state gain with 1/(dynamic gain) (8.36).
- **λ between 0 and 1: the period grows.** Solving the two-loop stability limit with equal dynamics gives the quadratic K_c1g_c1K₁₁g₁₁ = λ ± √(λ² − λ), i.e. **λ ± √λ(λ−1)** (8.37). For λ = 0.5 the roots are 0.707 ∠ ±135/−225 — complex, so loop phase is not −180° and **the period increases**; the longer period raises the process's dynamic gain, requiring a further gain cut. Simulated optimum: for λ = 0.5 both proportional bands and integral times must be **increased 64%** over single-loop optimum, and the relationship is roughly linear: **C = 0.22 + 0.78λ** (8.38), with all single-loop PID settings divided by C. Below λ = 0.5, I and D should be a little shorter than 8.38 implies for minimum combined IAE — but low-λ pairings should simply be avoided: they are very disturbance-sensitive and slow to recover even when tuned as well as possible. If the process is dead time without capacity, the band needs little change (dynamic gain is period-independent) but **integral time must vary inversely with λ**.
- **λ above 1: period unaffected, set point hurt.** With an even number of positive process signs the cross-loop is positive feedback, which adds no phase lag, so the period is unchanged and **no I and D correction is required**; P needs at most a halving for the whole range to infinity (take the smaller root of 8.37). Load response is superb — at λ = 10 both loops respond almost identically, and controller 1, which need not change its output in steady state, still integrates its deviation and drives m₁ to take nearly half the load, so short-term IAE is unchanged and long-term IAE is about double — still better than λ = 0.5. But **set-point response is stiff**: dc/dr = (2T/λ + 1)/(T²/λ + 2T + 1) (8.40), which reduces to the λ = 1 form only at unity, and the exponential settling time constant is proportional to λ. That matters when the interacting loops sit in the secondary of a cascade, where secondary set-point response is what makes primary load response work (the composition-to-temperature cascade on a column).
- **Negative λ: inverse response and conditional stability.** λ₁₁ < 0 implies K₁₂K₂₁ > K₁₁K₂₂ — the unintended positive-feedback path dominates the intended negative ones. Stability is possible by **reversing the action of one controller**, but then opening the other loop leaves only a positive-feedback path. The physical signature is **inverse response**: the controlled variable moves one way, then further the other way, because the indirect path goes through two blocks plus a control loop and arrives late. The inversion time is about half the damped period of the other loop; the reversed controller must be detuned in all modes, the other loop's band can go to half the single-loop value with I and D increased — so an optimally tuned negative-λ system is **conditionally unstable in both directions**. Control gets easier as λ becomes more negative, and λ → −∞ behaves like λ → +∞.
- **Dynamic coupling can justify a pairing the steady-state RGA rejects.** Paper-machine headbox: air valve has **zero steady-state gain** on total head (in steady state the falling level offsets the pressure rise), so its steady-state λ is 0 — yet the correct configuration is to control **total head with the air valve**, because total head must move before outflow can change and therefore responds faster than level. The dynamic relative gain is λ₁₁(τ0) = 1/√[1 + (K₂τ0/2πτ)²] ∠ tan⁻¹(K₂τ0/2πτ) (8.48), which approaches 1.0 as τ0/τ → 0. Simulation result: level on the air valve forces the level controller to a **200% band against 20%** for total head and makes both loops very sensitive to air-delivery upsets; the preferred pairing needs only 50% vs 30% and is far less sensitive to air upsets, with the liquid's share of total head making little difference.
- **Supply-side vs demand-side disturbances**: a supply upset enters through the manipulated variable's own block (fuel heating value) and must be countered by an opposite move of that same manipulated variable; a demand upset enters through both blocks and all manipulated variables must move. Supply-side upsets should be killed at the point of entry, by cascade, by regulating the load variable, or by *redefining* the manipulated variable (multiply fuel flow by heating value so the loop manipulates heat flow). Demand-side upsets need feedforward, often on several manipulated variables at once, each with its own gain and compensator.
- **Self-tuning in an interacting plant misreads the plant**: on a partially coupled system (K₁₂ = 0, so λ = 1 and no detuning is needed) a supply upset from loop 1 produces a lightly damped transient in loop 2. EXACT saw the light damping and **tripled its proportional band**, making the next genuine load change sluggish and overdamped, then spent several iterations recovering — and later reported "set-point change imposed on a load change" because the second peak was highest. One misleading disturbance is enough to corrupt the settings.
- **Relative disturbance gain (RDG)** complements λ for load response: β_i = (dm_i/dq)|c₁c₂ ÷ (dm_i/dq)|c₁,m₂ (8.49) — the manipulation required to hold both variables against the manipulation required to hold only one. Steady state: **β₁ = 1 − K₂qK₁₂/(K₂₂K₁q)** and **β₂ = 1 − K₁qK₂₁/(K₁₁K₂q)** (8.50–8.51), related by **β₂ = λ₁₁(1 − β₁)/(λ₁₁ − β₁)** (8.52). β = 1 means no help and no harm from interaction; **β < 1 means interaction improves load rejection**; β > 1 means it hurts. It gives IE (which depends on Δm), not IAE, and requires the detuning factor: IE_M/IE_S = β_i and the detuning factor for loop i is (P·D)_M/(P·I)_S = β_i (8.53–8.54). For distillation (Ref. 6): feed-composition upsets gave β = 0.17 / 0.82 for the two loops, feed-rate upsets gave −7.7 / 7.5. Two more criteria worth naming: ISE penalises large errors (worse for λ < 1), ITAE penalises long-lasting ones (worse for λ > 1).

## Decoupling
- **Essence**: a computing network that cancels the interaction so independent single-loop control is possible. The ideal decoupler is the process inverse — **not realisable**, since it would need leads and time advances to cancel lags and dead times, and even a steady-state decoupler brings operational and stability problems.
- **Input vs output decoupling**: input decoupling lets each controller move all valves so its own variable is unaffected; output decoupling generates a new set of controlled variables each responding to one manipulated variable only. Choose by which new variable set is more meaningful. Worked case (parallel reboilers): the levels interact severely while temperature responds to heat input; the working scheme **decouples the level measurements** — bottom-product flow controls the *sum* of the two levels (total inventory), and a difference controller biases the two hot-oil valves in opposite directions to balance levels, while the temperature controller moves both valves the same way. Properly calibrated, moving the bottom valve cannot change the level difference, and moving the oil valves differentially cannot change total heat input.
- **Decoupling multiple flow loops is just loop-gain compensation**: divide each controller output by √(pressure drop across its valve), which works for any number and any pressure disturbance. Non-linearities point the way: ratio (multiplication) for composition, sum/difference for level.
- **Two structures, very different behaviour**: decoupling the *controller outputs* (Fig. 8.16, m₁ = mc₁ + d₁₂m₂) makes initialization circular (mc₁ depends on the unknown mc₂) and — much worse — **breaks down under a constraint**: both controllers chase the one unconstrained variable and drive it to a limit. Feeding the decouplers *forward* from known m values (Fig. 8.17) initialises simply and keeps a constrained variable's signal useful as feedforward compensation, but introduces a feedback loop through the two decouplers. Its loop gain is the product of the decoupler gains: λ between 0.5 and 1 gives opposite signs (negative loop) and steady-state gain below 1 (stable); λ > 1 gives a positive loop with gain < 1 whose stability depends on g₁₂g₂₁ < g₁₁g₂₂ at all periods; **λ < 0 is unconditionally unstable**.
- **Decoupler error tolerance is the design limit**: real processes are nonlinear and time-variant, so fixed-gain decouplers only decouple exactly at one operating point. Deriving the decoupled relative gain λ₁₁d (8.62) and perturbing both decoupler gains by a common error δ gives **λ₁₁d = [1 − (λ₁₁ − 1)δ]²/[1 − (λ₁₁ − 1)δ(δ + 2)]** (8.65); λ₁₁d hits zero at δ = −λ₁₁/(λ₁₁ − 1) and **infinity at δ = λ₁₁/(λ₁₁ + 1)** (8.66–8.67). Negative errors (insufficient decoupling) push λ back toward unity, which is harmless for positive-λ loops; **positive errors (over-decoupling) destabilise loops with λ > 1**, and negative errors destabilise negative-λ loops. **No decoupler error can degrade a loop with λ between 0 and 1** — which is the design rule: prefer the 0-to-1 pairing when a choice exists. Table 8.2 gives the infinite-λ error margin: λ = 1.5 → 73.2%, 2 → 41.4%, 4 → 15.5%, 8 → 6.9%, 16 → 3.3% (negative λ: −1 → −29.3%, −2 → −18.4%, −5 → −8.7%, and imaginary for 0 < λ < 1).
- **Partial decoupling is usually the right answer**: implement one decoupler only. It breaks the decoupler feedback loop, keeps upsets out of the decoupled loop, avoids both instability paths, and is easier to tune — in fact its design and adjustment is identical to **feedforward control**. Which one to implement: the priority variable (decouple the important one), the constrained variable (d₁₂ keeps a constraint on m₂ from upsetting c₁ on a feedforward basis), or the speed asymmetry (a fast c₂ cannot be upset by the slow loop, so only the slow c₁ needs decoupling — the blending system, where the decoupler is a **multiplier** because composition depends on the ratio, not the sum; if level were the variable, a summer would be right).

## Mental Models
- **λ is a pairing score, not a difficulty score.** 0.5 interactive-but-workable, ∞ impossible, negative inverted, >1 workable with stiff set-point response.
- **Ask which regime you are in before you tune**: 0–1 costs period and needs C = 0.22 + 0.78λ on both P and I; >1 needs P halved at most and no I change; <0 needs an action reversal and is conditionally stable.
- **The steady-state RGA can be wrong about the right pairing** when the two loops differ in speed by an order of magnitude — the headbox is the proof, and the fix is the dynamic relative gain.
- **A constraint is what turns a clever decoupler into a hazard**: it opens a loop that the decoupling scheme assumed closed.
- **Prefer the 0-to-1 pairing**: it is the only regime where decoupler error cannot destabilise the loop.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pairing on the obvious valve because the algebra was skipped** — 1 in 6 odds at three loops.
- **Restricting manipulated variables to valve flows** — ratio and sum variables are legitimate and multiply the candidate configurations.
- **Detuning a slow loop to suit a fast partner, then opening the fast loop** — the slow loop's gain is then excessive and it cycles.
- **Applying a fixed-gain decoupler to a nonlinear process and assuming it holds across the range** — a large excursion can cross the error limit and destabilise, even though decoupling is perfect at the set point.
- **Adding a decoupler between a slow composition loop and a fast flow loop** — the fast loop cannot be upset by the slow one, so that decoupler only adds risk.
- **Expecting a self-tuning controller to sort out loop interaction** — it misreads coupled transients, and one bad disturbance corrupts the settings it derived from several good ones.

## Worked Example
**Two valves in series controlling pressure and flow (Ex. 8.1)**: with h = m₁m₂(p₀−p₂)/(m₁+m₂) and p₁ the intermediate pressure, the RGA comes out λ₁₁ = (p₀−p₁)/(p₀−p₂) and λ_h2 = (p₁−p₂)/(p₀−p₂) — all entries between 0 and 1 (typical when the controlled variables differ while the manipulated variables are the same). The rule that falls straight out: **give flow control to the valve with the larger pressure drop**, and if p₁ sits midway between p₀ and p₂ every element is 0.5, so the pairing genuinely does not matter. That is the whole value of the analysis — it converts a coin flip into a number.
