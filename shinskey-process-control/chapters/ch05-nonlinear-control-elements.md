# Chapter 5: Nonlinear Control Elements

## Core Idea
In a linear loop the loop gain is constant, so every disturbance gets the same damping. In a nonlinear loop, gain varies with amplitude, and the direction of that variation decides everything: **gain falling with amplitude produces a limit cycle that no tuning can remove**, while gain rising with amplitude means small upsets are heavily damped but a big enough upset can cross the point of no return. So nonlinearity is not merely a nuisance to be compensated — it is also a design tool, and the whole chapter is about telling the harmful nonlinearities (dead band, velocity limiting, negative resistance) from the useful ones (limiters, dead zones, error-squared and three-piece gain shaping).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Describing functions**: characterize a nonlinear element's gain and phase against a sinusoidal input. Filtering by the loop's lags leaves the fundamental, so a square wave's sinusoidal equivalent is **4/π** times its amplitude, a triangular wave's is **8/π²** times. Measure a real element's function in closed loop by reducing P until it limit-cycles — loop gain is then exactly 1.0, giving **G_N = 1/(K_c G_i) = P·2πτ_i/(100·τ0)** (5.3), with the element's phase from **φ_N = −90 − 360τd/τ0** (5.4) when dead time and an integrator make up the rest of the loop. For a gain that *rises* with amplitude, replace the proportional controller with integral action and remove the dead time to force a cycle.
- **Limiter (saturation)**: passes a sine undistorted below the limit, then clips it. Gain ≈ a/A but distorted, so the true describing function is G_L = (2/π)[sin⁻¹α + α√(1−α²)], α = sin⁻¹(a/A) (5.6); as a/A → 0 the output becomes square and G_L → (4/π)(a/A). Gain falling with amplitude ⇒ **capable of limit cycling**, which is also why every expanding cycle ends at a valve limit. Applied deliberately to a controller input, a limiter set just inside the point of no return stabilized a loop better than widening the band, because **gain to small disturbances was untouched**.
- **Dead zone**: removes the centre of the signal; G_z = (2/π)(...)/cos α with α = sin⁻¹(z/A) (5.7). Gain *increases* with amplitude but never exceeds 1.0, so it cannot promote an expanding cycle. Caution: in a non-self-regulating process, or one with a long time constant and high gain (pH), the signal will not sit in the dead zone at all — it drifts into a live zone, comes to rest at a corner, or limit-cycles between corners. Tune such loops at high amplitude.
- **Dead band (square-loop hysteresis)**: unlike a dead zone, the path differs going up and down — friction in valve packing and guides. Phase lag ≈ **φ_b = −sin⁻¹(a/A)** (5.8), *increasing as amplitude falls*, so the period lengthens as a cycle decays, and with two integrators in the loop (a level process plus a PI controller) loop gain rises as amplitude falls and a limit cycle appears. Remedies: remove the integral mode, close a loop round the nonlinearity (valve positioner, cascade flow loop), or inject dither at a frequency above the loop's and an amplitude above a.
- **Velocity limiting**: the actuator's stroking speed caps the rate, so the element tracks small signals faster than large ones. When the wave's peak velocity 2πA/τ0 (5.9) exceeds the limit u, the output becomes a triangular wave with **G_u = 2uτ0/(πA)** (5.10) and **φ_u = −90 + sin⁻¹(uτ0/(...))** (5.11). Phase lag *increases* with amplitude, so large excursions cycle at a longer period — the opposite of dead band. Tune for the large-amplitude case and damping is guaranteed as amplitude falls into the linear regime; the cost is a longer period during recovery, so **increase I, never D**.
- **Negative resistance**: a region where the process has no steady state and cannot be driven manually. The worked case is steam-jet ejector vacuum (negative resistance between 10 and 30 torr, positive resistance outside): outflow falls as pressure rises, so f_0 = b − kp, giving a process whose phase lies **between −90° and −180°** (5.12) while its dynamic gain still looks like a first-order lag's. Controlling it inside that region requires the controller's steady-state negative feedback to exceed the process's positive feedback, which caps the proportional band from above: **P < 100/k** (5.13). The lower bound for dynamic stability is **P > 100·τ₁/(k·τn)** (5.14), and the two bounds can only be satisfied if **τd < τ₁** (5.15) — in practice τd should be below 0.4τ₁, keeping the natural period under 5τd. Because gain and period are both worse in the negative-resistance regime, a fixed tuning is too loose elsewhere: schedule the settings on the characteristic's local slope, or use a self-tuning controller. Note the exception this creates: **a process that cannot be controlled manually can still be controllable automatically.** Transition boiling (temperature difference above ~40°F/22°C on the heat-transfer surface) is the same phenomenon with a slope too steep for automatic control at all.

## On-off controllers
- **Two-state on an integrating process**: the controlled variable ramps up at (m₁ − q)/τ₁ and down at (m₀ − q)/τ₁, and switching is delayed by τd, giving a triangular limit cycle of amplitude **A_n = 0.5·τd[(m₁ − q) + (q − m₀)]/τ₁** (5.18) at the natural period **τn = 4τd**. Keep m₁ − m₀ as narrow as the load range allows, and if the load is not midway the cycle sits asymmetrically — a sawtooth of the same amplitude and period.
- **Real on-off with dead band a**: amplitude **A₀ = A_n + a** (5.19) and period **τ0 = τ_p·(A₀/A_n)** (5.20). With no dead time at all, the cycle amplitude equals the dead band and the period follows **τ0 = 2aτ₁[1/(m₁ − q) + 1/(q − m₀)]** (5.21), shortest at mid-range load.
- **Proportional-time control**: modulate the on-off output with a fixed period and an "on" time proportional to deviation — the average output equals what a proportional controller would give. The loop then limit-cycles at the modulation period τm, which must be chosen long enough that process gain at that period is small; the loop then behaves like proportional control.
- **Three-state (reversible motor)**: two on-off devices separated by a dead zone z, each with its own dead band a. The dead zone exists first to stop the windings from being energized together, and second to give the loop a rest state so it need not limit-cycle. Stability requires **z + a > 100·K_p·τd/τ₁** (5.22); if that fails, the cycle amplitude is **A = ±100K_p·τd/τ₁ − z** (5.23) at τn = 4τd (or **τ0 = 4τd + t_d** (5.24) when a > z). Offset can be as large as a, so both z and a should be about half the value required by 5.22. Two cautions: the valve motor is itself an integrator, so this scheme cannot control a non-self-regulating process; and on a process with a long time constant the dead zone needed for stability can make the offset unacceptable — fix it with a proportional-time controller, a superimposed PID (poor, because valve + controller double-integrate), or best, **feedback from valve position or flow**, which converts the valve from an integrator into a lag and makes the scheme usable on non-self-regulating processes.

## Dual-mode control (the answer to "what would you want if anything were possible")
- **The four demands**: maximum speed, critical damping, no offset, insensitivity to noise. Each demand maps to a mode — speed requires saturation for any measurable deviation (on-off), critical damping must be confined to a narrow band about set point, zero offset requires an integrator, noise immunity requires low gain to small signals. The combination that reconciles them is **high gain to large signals, low gain to small signals**, which no single controller delivers — hence two controllers in sequence.
- **Within the band, use a PID**; outside it, on-off. Nonlinearity helps set-point response (big steps) more than load response (errors that originate and subside inside the band), which is exactly why the linear controller owns the small-signal region.
- **Optimal switching** (minimum-time): for dead-time + integrating processes, switch m from 100% to the load q when the intermediate variable reaches set point, with the deviation at switching **e_i = −(100% − q)τd/τ₁** (5.25) and the reverse-direction counterpart **e_h = −q·τd/τ₁** (5.26); their separation is the dead zone **z = e_i + e_h = 100·τd/τ₁** (5.27), analogous to a proportional band.
- **Two-capacity refinement**: keep 100% output τd longer, then drop to 0% for a timed interval. For q = 50%: **t_d = 0.693τ₂** and **e_i = 15.35τ₂/τ₁** (5.28). With dead time added, **t_d = τ₂·ln[100/(100 − q)]** and **e_i = [100(τ₂ + τd) − q(τ₂ + τd + t_d)]/τ₁** (5.29), numerically solvable even for a three-element process.
- **Adding the linear controller**: program the switching exactly as if there were no linear controller, preload the PID to m_q, and use two timers — the first transfers output to the PID (still at preload), the second puts the PID in automatic after the process has come to rest. In cascade (e.g. reactor temperature to jacket temperature) the first delay is essentially unnecessary. Seven adjustments, in two independent groups (PID for the steady state, program for the transient). Diagnostics are unambiguous: wrong e_i ⇒ overshoot/undershoot; t_d too long ⇒ the variable turns back down after set point; wrong m_q ⇒ a bump after the first delay. The second delay is not critical.

## Nonlinear PID controllers
- **Error-squared controller**: a continuous, adjustable version of the same idea, with gain f(|e|) = [L + (1 − L)|e|]/100 · 1/P (5.30–5.31) — L = 1 is linear, L → 0 is square-law. Do not let L reach 0 or small-signal gain vanishes and offset returns; **L ≈ 0.1** gives a minimum gain of 10/P. With the gain curve superimposed on the zero-damping and quarter-amplitude contours, three regions appear: heavier-than-quarter-amplitude damping near zero (reject noise, kill small upsets slowly), lighter damping (fast recovery) on either side, and beyond that instability — so **the proportional band must be set for the largest anticipated deviation**. Set-point response beats a linear PID; small load changes are handled worse, since the linear controller has more gain near set point.
- **Gain×integral-time fixed variant**: I shortened as gain rises, giving constant damping — devised for averaging level control (minimal outflow movement for small inflow upsets, tight enough when upsets are large). **Do not use it on boilers, reboilers or other thermally/hydraulically loaded vessels**: its dynamic gain rises with the *square* of deviation, so one large disturbance can push loop gain past unity and produce an expanding oscillation — and that only has to happen once to lose the operators' confidence. The fixed-integral-time version carries the same risk in lesser degree.
- **Three-piece nonlinear controller for pH**: the titration curve is too severe for the parabolic form, so use a low-gain zone of gain **k_z** and width **z** (Fig. 5.27) flanked by high-gain zones. k_z must not be zero: too low and pH limit-cycles between the two high-gain zones at a period far longer than natural; too high and a smaller cycle appears near the natural period. Field result (Fig. 5.28): with z = ±2.2 pH and k_z = 0.05, the proportional band could be **14× lower** than a linear controller tuned to avoid cycling, and integral time only needed to be ~30% longer. If the set point must sit off-centre, delete one high-gain region and extend the low-gain one.
- **Noise filtering is a free benefit**: because the function rejects small-amplitude signals, a nonlinear PI controller is an efficient filter on a noisy flow loop — smoother valve motion, more stable loop, and it works on periodic (pulsating) disturbances as well as random ones.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tuning a dead-band loop tighter** — widening the band of an integrating process under PI *increases* cycle amplitude and lengthens the period; increasing I reduces amplitude but lengthens the period. Remove the integrator or close a loop around the valve.
- **Assuming a limit cycle can be tuned away** — it cannot; reducing controller gain only moves the amplitude at which loop gain crosses unity.
- **Using a three-state motorized valve on a non-self-regulating process** without valve-position or flow feedback — the motor adds the second integrator and the loop cannot rest.
- **Setting an error-squared controller's band from the noise level** — it must be set for the largest anticipated deviation, or the high-gain region becomes an instability.
- **Treating "cannot be controlled manually" as "uncontrollable"** — steam-jet ejector vacuum is the counterexample, controllable within τd/τ₁ < 1.

## Worked Example
**PI on an integrating process with a dead-banded valve** (Tables 5.1–5.2): proportional control alone is stable at every amplitude (gain 0.50 → 0.40 as the cycle shrinks), so the dead band does not hurt. Add integral action tuned sensibly at an amplitude ratio of 4.0 and the same element now *raises* loop gain as the cycle decays — 0.50 at A/a = 4, 0.57 at 3.5, 1.18 at 3.3 — so the loop settles into a limit cycle at roughly twice the period it showed at 4.0. Nothing about the controller settings caused this; the **second integrator** did. That is the diagnostic that separates "mistuned" from "structurally nonlinear", and the fix is structural: drop the integral mode or close a positioner loop around the valve.
