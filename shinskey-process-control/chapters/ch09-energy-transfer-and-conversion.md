# Chapter 9: Energy Transfer and Conversion

## Core Idea
Work is the superior form of energy and control decisions should avoid degrading it into heat, but the control problems here are otherwise familiar ones wearing thermal clothes. Heat transfer is intrinsically **self-regulating** (temperature difference drives the rate, and the rate changes the difference), so the difficulty is not stability but **nonlinearity**: gain moves with flow ratio, temperature set point and flow rate, and dead time moves with flow. Phase change removes most of that trouble (latent heat makes mass flow a direct measure of heat flow), combustion and steam plant add safety-selective logic, and machines (pumps, compressors) add an efficiency argument that determines *which* variable to manipulate — speed beats throttling, and for a centrifugal compressor the choice is forced by surge.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Direct-contact heating (blending)**: Wh + Wc = W (9.1), enthalpy balance (9.2), giving (T − Tc)/(Th − Tc) = 1/(R + 1) with R = Wh/Wc (9.4) — a **hyperbolic** relation between controlled temperature and manipulated flow ratio, with gain varying with both set point and flow, and mixing-to-sensor dead time varying with flow. Relative gain of temperature to hot flow is λ = (Th − T)/(Th − Tc) / ... = 1/(R+1) (9.5), so the flow and temperature loops interact, worst when one valve carries most of the flow. **Decouple with a three-way mixing valve for temperature plus a two-way valve in the common line for flow** — with check valves on both supply lines so that at low demand one fluid cannot back into the other.
- **Exchangers without phase change**: Q = UA·ΔTm (9.6) with ΔTm the log-mean difference (9.7); control can act on U, A, or ΔTm. Normalised solution for the general counterflow case: (Tc2 − Tc1)/(Th1 − Tc1) = (1 − ε)/(R − ε) (9.11), (Th2 − Tc1)/(Th1 − Tc1) = (1 − R)/(1/ε − R) (9.12), with ε = exp(UA/WcCc − UA/WhCh). Indeterminate at R = 1, continuous, and simply solvable there (9.11a–9.12a). All curves are strongly nonlinear: **little further temperature rise beyond WhCh/UA = 1** (Fig. 9.4).
  - The load-following numbers show why: with flows matched at 1 and half-rise controlled, dropping cold flow to 0.5 requires hot flow to fall to **0.2**; and *doubling* cold flow puts the required temperature beyond what the hot stream can deliver (its maximum rise is only 0.39). Maximum heat transfer is approached only by infinite manipulated flow, i.e. the precious surface is fully used exactly where control is least effective.
  - Allowing U to vary with velocity (both film coefficients ∝ velocity^0.8, barrier resistance neglected) makes the result depend on the flow **ratio** R (Fig. 9.6) and promises ratio-style load regulation — but the real characteristic lies between the two models, so the manipulated flow must change more than the load flow.
  - Practical rules: **throttle the cleaner stream** where fouling matters (river water deposits); otherwise bypass with a three-way diverting/mixing valve for rangeability a simple bypass cannot give (or two valves, both half-open at 50% output, one closing as the other opens). Bypassing does not improve linearity but **keeps dead time short and constant**, and without it an exit-temperature loop **limit-cycles**: a low temperature increases flow (shortening dead time, fast correction) while a high temperature reduces flow (lengthening dead time, sluggish correction), so period and gain both wander with load. When flow must be manipulated, choose an **equal-percentage** characteristic.
- **Phase change transforms the problem**: Q = W·Hv (9.16), and the boiling/condensing medium's temperature barely changes through the exchanger. Steam flow to a process heater is therefore an accurate measure of heat rate; steam pressure only indicates steam temperature (useful for estimating U, not for heat measurement). Condensing-steam film coefficients dominate, so heat transfer varies nearly linearly with process-fluid velocity — meaning the exit temperature is largely self-regulating, and **cascading process temperature to steam pressure** works very well. Manipulating a **valve in the condensate line** (flooding) changes heat transfer with a much smaller valve but responds more slowly.
  - Condensing a vapour: total condensation requires condensate temperature control (constant pressure) or vapour-pressure control (closed vessel); Q = WcCc(Tv − Tci)(1 − e^(−UA/WcCc)) (9.17). Bypassing vapour is not effective unless it floods the condenser — where heat transfer is governed by exposed area, and condensing coefficients exceed those for subcooling, which is the only reason level can control pressure at all. Flooded exchangers are **non-self-regulating** (they depend on liquid level), so tune like level loops; 90° of lag from level to area is acceptable because vapour pressure responds fast, and a valve positioner helps.
  - Air-cooled condensers: coolant-side manipulations (louvers, variable-pitch fans, variable-speed drives, in ascending cost and energy-saving capability; two-speed motors to extend range) versus process-side manipulations (flooding, bypassing) which are more satisfactory for control but ignore the energy opportunity.
- **Combustion**: heat released = Wf·Hc (9.18) must equal the sensible heat of the products (9.19), which solves for flame temperature T = (Hc + CfTf + KaCaTa)/(Cf + KaCa) (9.20) — valid only with no excess fuel. Nitrogen dilutes; replacing air with oxygen cuts Ka fivefold with a large effect on flame temperature; the calculated temperature overstates the measured one because of ionisation (recovered on recombination).
  - **Flame temperature is a poor controlled variable** (it falls with either excess *or* deficient air). Use **oxygen in the flue gas** as the efficiency index: natural gas ≈ 5% excess air (0.9% excess O2), oil 6% (1.1%), coal 10% (1.9%). Radiation varies with the fourth power of absolute flame temperature, so maximum furnace efficiency coincides with maximum flame temperature, but excess air redistributes heat — lower near the burner, higher further away.
  - **Safety dictates the structure**: fuel must never exceed what the available air supports. In the fuel-air control system, the ratio is adjusted by manipulating the **span** of the air measurement from the oxygen controller rather than its set point, precisely so that both controllers can carry the same set point and the selector logic works: if airflow is lost, the air measurement is preferentially selected to *lower* the fuel set point; if fuel flow is excessive, its measurement is selected to *raise* air. That protects against fan failure, controller failure, and set-point lags. CO analysers cost more, are less reliable, and are usually monitoring only (they can reveal downstream air infiltration or uneven burners).
  - Airflow versus furnace draft interacts; resolve it with a **decoupler** in which the flow controller manipulates both forced- and induced-draft dampers, while the pressure controller trims the exhaust damper through a multiplier. A dynamic compensator is needed if pressure doesn't respond equally fast through both paths.
- **Fired heaters**: fuel rate sets exit temperature, air is inspirated proportionally. Dead time of minutes (hundreds of feet of tubing) varying with flow, so use feedforward (same heat balance as the exchanger with Hc in place of Hv), or — more common — **cascade from product temperature to convection-zone/stack temperature**, which responds far faster to firing rate. Stack losses vary directly with load and can be absorbed by feedback trim. Variable refinery fuel-gas composition (hydrogen, light hydrocarbons) upsets heat transfer and flue composition; a gas-density sensor or calorimeter can compensate, but a calorimeter may be no faster than the heater itself.
- **Steam plant fundamentals**: steam is valued for **available work**, which increases with the logarithm of pressure and with temperature; throttling steam through a valve for pressure reduction superheats it and destroys available work, so avoid it. Flow measurement with an orifice needs density correction: W = k√(h·p) (9.21) for saturated steam (pressure linear with density, intercept 1 atm). For **superheated** steam the useful simplification is that temperature's effect on enthalpy offsets its effect on density, so thermal power depends only on differential and static pressure: **Q = kH0√(h·p)** (9.22).
- **Drum level: inverse response by design.** Feedwater cooler than the drum water condenses steam; a feedwater increase momentarily *reduces* boiling, the supporting bubbles collapse, and **level falls before it rises**. Characterised as a lag with a **negative lead**: gain and phase (9.23–9.24), and for τ1 = τ2 = τi, G = 1.0 at all periods with φi = −2tan⁻¹(2πτi/τ0) (9.25) — pure phase lag, no attenuation. Combined with the integrating element, the natural period is where φ = −90°, i.e. **τn = 2πτi ≈ 3.94·ti** (9.26–9.28) using the measured **inversion time** (the time for the step response to recross its starting point, ti = 1.594τi from 9.27). So inverse response behaves like dead time of about 1.59τi with a natural period ≈ 4× the inversion time — which explains why drum level cycles far slower than hydraulic resonance or instrument lags would suggest. Compared with equivalent dead time: **PID does 25% better on inverse response, PI does 30% worse** — but drum-level noise forbids derivative, so inverse response ends up the harder element, and model-based control also fares worse with it than with dead time.
  - **Shrink and swell**: a falling drum pressure flashes water in the tubes and swells the level; a rising pressure shrinks it. The traditional answer is to ignore them by letting the forward loop carry the load with loose settings (**P near 100%, several minutes of integral time**). The better answer is dynamic compensation: subtract the derivative of steam flow (or drum pressure, signs reversed) from the level measurement into the controller (Fig. 9.12), lag ≈ 15 s. Where applied it allowed the level controller's band to be **cut by 3–4×**, which opens the door to proportional-only level control with feedforward through the path normally used for integral feedback; if integral action is needed, use the Fig. 7.2 arrangements.
- **Drum pressure**: pressure measures stored energy; steam leaves at the user's demand, so pressure can only be held if firing rate matches withdrawal. Firing on *steam flow alone* creates positive feedback — what is needed is a **demand** signal, obtainable as Q = a·p (9.29), giving the feedforward index from combining with 9.22 (**a ∝ √(h·p)·p / H0... the "power demand" signal**, Eq. 9.30). Behaviour is exactly the supply/demand distinction of Chap. 8: a demand increase raises flow and lowers pressure, so the index calls for more firing and returns to the steady steam flow when pressure recovers; a **supply-side** upset (fuel delivery or heating value) moves flow and pressure in the *same* direction, leaving the index unchanged — so it must be caught by feedback on fuel flow or steam pressure. That is the chronic problem with solid fuels, where flow is hard to measure and moisture varies.
- **Steam temperature**: control by shifting heat between superheater and evaporating surfaces (tilting burners, flue-gas recirculation/bypass); attemperation with feedwater is the trim, but it bypasses evaporating surface and so wastes available work. The control structure minimises attemperating water while staying responsive: superheater-outlet temperature cascades to *inlet* temperature, with the secondary measurement used as feedback for the primary's integral mode (no windup at low load where high temperature cannot be held); inlet temperature control is **split** — P and D go to the attemperator valve (which returns to a fixed position at zero deviation, set by the controller's bias) while the PI modes move burners/dampers for long-term control.
- **Machines**: choose the manipulation that preserves work.
  - Positive-displacement pumps must always be able to discharge their rated flow — a relief valve from discharge to suction is mandatory, and flow is controlled by a bypass (or by regulating discharge pressure with a bypass). Metering pumps (adjustable stroke or speed) serve where accuracy and high pressures are needed but must be free of leakage and particulates, and vapour-lock if the fluid has entrained gas. **DC variable-speed drives stall below 5% and do not restart until 10%**, producing a sawtooth limit cycle at low plant load; most variable-frequency AC drives do not have this limitation.
  - Centrifugal pumps: head-flow contours parabolic in speed and flow, Δp/ρ = k₁N² − k₂F² (9.31); load lines Δp_q/ρ = k₃F² (9.33). Hydraulic horsepower hhp = F·Δp/1714 (9.32, gpm and psi; replace 1714 with 98 for m³/s and kg/cm²). At constant speed, throttling from 200 to 100 gpm barely changes power, but reducing speed at constant load **cuts power by a factor of 3**; with zero static head, hhp varies with the **cube** of flow, and flow is exactly linear with speed (9.35). Reported field result: frequency control of the induction motor gave a flow-loop band as narrow as **30%** and rangeability of at least 100:1 — far better than a throttling valve. Turbines, magnetic and hydraulic couplings sit between, and all beat throttling or recirculating discharge to suction.
  - Compressors: compressing a gas and then cooling it destroys the work irrecoverably (an ideal gas's energy does not depend on pressure), so keep the pressure rise no higher than the process needs, cool any recycle gas, and minimise recycle flow. Reciprocating machines use cylinder unloaders or clearance pockets (on-off, sequenced with a bypass valve per the multi-unit scheme; bypass should be linear, fail-open, positioned); variable speed is more efficient where practicable.
  - **Centrifugal compressors and surge**: the compression-ratio/flow curve shows **negative resistance** below the surge line — compression ratio falls, flow falls further, discharge gas flows back, and the cycle repeats in under a second. White's surge line relates adiabatic head to the square of volumetric suction flow (9.36); substituting the orifice relation (9.37) makes suction temperature and molecular weight cancel, leaving **h = K(pd − ps)** (9.38) — a straight line in measurable variables. In practice plot actual (pd − ps) against h, draw the best straight line to the **right of all data points**, and calibrate the antisurge controller to that line (Fig. 9.18). The antisurge flow controller must be protected against windup and the bypass valve must be linear and fail-open.
  - **The bypass valve is the only correct surge manipulator**: its load line rotates about the origin, nearly parallel to the surge curve, so its effect is normal to the surge line; changing speed moves *along* the load line and barely affects the margin (Fig. 9.20). Relative gain for that pair is slightly above 1.0; if troublesome, decouple by biasing the pressure controller output with bypass-valve position. The practical obstacle is locating the surge curve accurately — composition, compression ratio and machine condition all move it, so a method comparing volumetric flow to machine speed may be needed, and adjustable inlet guide vanes mean a different curve for every vane position.
  - **Asymmetric antisurge tuning** (Fehervari): P and I on the safe side of the control line **10× larger** than on the unsafe side. Below the line, the aggressive settings overreact and drive flow back across set point; above it, the loose settings give an overdamped, overshoot-free return. It also self-corrects for slight set-point misplacement, because rising surge-related flow noise gets **rectified** by the asymmetric loop, offsetting the controlled flow toward the safe side in proportion to the noise. Setting safe-side P and I at twice optimum allows unsafe-side values of one-fifth optimum — essentially the highest tolerable gain, producing a large rebound after a small unsafe excursion but rapid recovery, and short-lived transients for safe-direction upsets.

## Mental Models
- **Heat transfer is self-regulating; its sin is nonlinearity.** Ask what the gain does with flow ratio and set point before choosing a valve characteristic.
- **Phase change is a gift**: latent heat turns a flow measurement into a heat measurement and flattens the temperature profile.
- **Dead time that moves with flow is worse than dead time that is merely large** — it produces a limit cycle, and the cure is bypassing rather than tuning.
- **A drum's level is a lie during transients.** Inverse response, swell and shrink all mean the level signal is not inventory; compensating the measurement is worth more than any tuning.
- **Buy work, not pressure.** Throttling a pump or compressor converts work into heat; speed control keeps it.
- **For surge, the shape of the load line decides the manipulator** — and the antisurge loop should be asymmetric because the cost of the two directions is asymmetric.

## Anti-patterns
- **Throttling a discharge valve to control a positive-displacement pump** — it will exceed its pressure rating; bypass instead, and never operate one without a relief path.
- **Using flame temperature as the combustion controlled variable** — it falls on both sides of stoichiometric.
- **Controlling fuel-air ratio by adjusting the oxygen controller's set point** when the safety selector needs both controllers at the same set point — adjust the air-measurement span instead.
- **Manipulating a compressor's speed for surge protection** — it moves along the load line and does not restore margin.
- **Attemperating freely for steam-temperature control** — it bypasses evaporating surface and destroys available work.
- **Throttling steam to reduce pressure** — same loss of available work.
- **Reducing a drum-level proportional band without compensating shrink and swell** — the loop is being asked to control a measurement that is not inventory.

## Worked Example
**Drum boiler level, τdrum = 50 s, measured inversion time ti = 12 s**: τi = ti/1.594 = 7.5 s, so the natural period is τn ≈ 3.94 × 12 = **47 s** — long enough to explain a level loop that cycles far slower than any instrument lag would suggest, and short enough that the loop can still be made fast. Optimum PI settings scale off the integrator time constant: P = 150τi/τI and I = 9τi, which for a 50-s drum gives I ≈ 68 s. Then compare the two paths: if the same dynamic difficulty had come from dead time instead, PID would have been 25% better on IAE — but drum-level noise forbids derivative, so PI is what must be tuned, and it is 30% worse than the dead-time equivalent. That arithmetic is why drum level is traditionally run loose (P near 100%) with feedforward carrying the load, and why the shrink/swell compensator (which let the band shrink 3–4×) is the real improvement.
