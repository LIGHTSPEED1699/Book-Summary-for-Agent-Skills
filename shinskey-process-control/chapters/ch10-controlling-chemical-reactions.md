# Chapter 10: Controlling Chemical Reactions

## Core Idea
A reactor's control problem is decided before any instrument is chosen: by its **equilibrium**, its **kinetics**, and above all by the **sign of its thermal self-regulation**. An equilibrium constant caps conversion and says which way to push temperature, pressure and excess reactant. A first-order rate law makes the reactor either a dead-time element (plug flow) or a single capacity (back-mixed). But the decisive fact is that an exothermic reactor is a **positive feedback loop** — heat evolves, temperature rises, rate rises — and whether that loop can be closed by control depends entirely on how much heat-transfer area is provided against the reaction's own thermal gain. Shinskey's rule is blunt: **reactors can be designed to be uncontrollable**, and the control engineer's first job is to recognise that before trying to tune it. Conversion, selectivity and production rate are then second-order optimisations, mostly achieved by manipulating **reactor severity** (temperature) against a recycle inventory, with everything else at a constraint.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Equilibrium and the four tactics that defeat it.** Reversible reactions (SO2 + ½O2 ⇌ SO3 + heat, 10.1) stop at K = [SO3]/([SO2][O2]^0.5) (10.2), a function of temperature and catalyst. Because that ratio is fixed, some reactants must always leave with the product — but conversion can be pushed by: (1) **removing product** from the reaction zone by condensation, which can approach 100% if products separate as gas/solid from a liquid reaction or as liquid/solid from a vapour reaction; (2) **excess of a reactant**, since SO3 depends on both SO2 and O2, so either may be run rich to convert the other; (3) **pressure**, when the moles of reactant exceed the moles of product (this reaction has 1.5 vs 1), which forces the numerator up; and (4) **heat removal**, because high temperature favours the reverse reaction.
- **Reactors as dynamic elements.** Irreversible first-order kinetics with x the controlling reactant: −dx/dt = kx (10.5), a reactant's concentration decays exponentially in time with a **time constant 1/k**, so k carries units of inverse time. Conversion y = 1 − e^(−kt) (10.6).
  - **Plug flow** = dead time, residence V/F: y = 1 − e^(−kV/F) (10.7), exit concentration x = x0·e^(−kV/F).
  - **Back-mixed** = a single capacity: exit concentration x = x0/(1 + kV/F), conversion y = kV/F/(1 + kV/F) (10.8), and from the unsteady balance the concentration time constant is **τx = V/(F + Vk)** (10.9) — a function of kinetics as well as residence time.
  - Plumbing them together: plug flow always gives **higher conversion** than back-mixing at the same conditions and V/F, because back-mixing dilutes the feed immediately. The maximum slope of the conversion-temperature curve always occurs at **kV/F = 1** — 63% conversion in plug flow, 50% in back-mixed.
- **Arrhenius decides everything that is hard.** k = a·e^(−E/RT) (10.10). For a typical reaction (a = e²⁹ min⁻¹, E/R = 20,000 °R) the rate coefficient climbs steeply with temperature, so conversion in a plug-flow reactor varies with temperature as a **double exponential**.
  - The **steady-state** slopes are dy/dT = (E/RT²)(1 − y)ln(1 − y) for plug flow (10.11) and dy/dT = (E/RT²)(1 − y)y for back-mixed (10.12). For V/F = 1 these peak at 1/64.7°F and 1/95°F respectively at 230°F.
  - The **dynamic** slope is steeper, because a heat-transfer upset moves k before it moves concentration: the extra instantaneous conversion per unit temperature is the steady-state result divided by (1 − y): dy/dT = (E/RT²)ln(1 − y) for plug flow (10.14) and **dy/dT = (E/RT²)·y** for back-mixed (10.15). The dynamic slope is what determines stability, and it is the one that bites.
- **Stability of exothermic reactors — the central design question.** Heat evolution Qr = Hr·F·x0·y (10.16) rises with temperature along the same conversion curve; heat removal Qt = UA(T − Tc) is a straight line of slope UA from the coolant temperature. **Steady-state stability requires the slope of the heat-removal line to exceed the slope of the heat-evolution curve at the operating point.** With insufficient area there are three intersections: two stable (L and N, a cold and a hot state) and one unstable between them — the reactor latches onto either runaway or extinction, and the operating point in the middle is unreachable.
  - The unsteady heat balance HrFx0y − UA(T − Tc) − FρC(T − TF) = VρC·dT/dt (10.17) contains the sensible-heat term (third term) and the thermal capacity (right side). Solving for the manipulated variable and differentiating gives the **thermal steady-state gain**
    - **Kt = dT/dTc = −UA / [UA + FρC − HrFx0(dy/dT)x]** (10.20)
  - and the **thermal time constant** τt = Kt·VρC/UA (10.21). Three regimes follow directly, and they are the mental model for the whole chapter: **Kt positive → self-regulating first-order lag**; **Kt → ∞ → non-self-regulating capacity** with τt = VρC/UA; **Kt negative → steady-state unstable**, i.e. a negative-resistance process with **negative τt**.
  - The unstable reactor has the phase lag of negative resistance — −180° at steady state, rising toward −90° as period shortens — so its limits are those derived in Chap. 5:
    - **Steady-state stability requires P < −100Kt** (10.22).
    - **Dynamic stability requires P > 100Kt·τd/τt** (10.23).
  - The two requirements squeeze the proportional band from both sides, and the margin closes as **τd/τt → −1.0**. In practice a negative thermal time constant less than half the dead time (**τd/τt < −0.5**) should be regarded as **uncontrollable**, even though the algebra technically allows it. Unstable reactors also exhibit **two resonant peaks** — a fast one near 3τd and a second near **9τd** that grows as stability worsens; widening the band trades one for the other.
- **Recognising and rescuing an uncontrollable reactor.** The equation says exactly what to do, because Kt and τt depend on the same quantities: **reduce production rate F, reduce feed concentration x0, or increase UA**. Practical forms of "increase UA": use a **boiling liquid coolant** instead of a circulating one (much higher coefficient — cheaper than adding surface), or boil a volatile component of the reaction mass, condense it and return it as reflux, using the condenser as extra area (this is how an uncontrollable reactor can be made self-regulating or non-self-regulating and therefore controllable-with-tight-tuning). Three free wins once it is marginal: keep **coolant flow at maximum** to minimise dead time, **cascade reactor temperature to coolant temperature** for speed, and **use derivative in both controllers**.
- **Continuous reactors: single-pass vs recycle** — the classification that determines which loops exist (single-pass; recycle of reactant, of inert vehicle, or of product as moderator). The control consequences:
  - **Apportioning reactant flows.** When a reactant differs in phase from products and other reactants, add it as consumed by **controlling its inventory** in the reactor. Purge is an absolute necessity, not a nicety: nitrogen in a hydrogen feed or lubricating oil in a liquid dead end accumulates until it impedes the reaction.
  - **Ratio control is paramount in single-pass** reactions (e.g. neutralisation): excess reactant is wasted and may cause side reactions or corrosion, and manually set ratio is not accurate enough if a stream's composition varies. Use an **endpoint analyser for feedback trim**.
  - **Recycled reactants are never critical** (always in excess) so fresh reactant can be added by inventory control. **Recycled product or vehicle changes everything**: with solvent recycle and no concentration loop, an imbalance accumulates without limit. The mass balance gives x1 = (X − Y)/V·∫dt (10.24) — **the process becomes non-self-regulating**, which **rules out integral control on composition** (an integrating controller on a non-self-regulating process winds up) and therefore rules out any feedback-optimising scheme. Endpoint feedback is essential instead.
- **Limits inside the loop: integral action must be paired with the right measurement.** The above is the mechanical reason the rest of the book keeps insisting that non-self-regulating processes are controlled by proportional or PD action only.
- **Temperature control of exothermic reactors.**
  - **Initiation is a two-way problem**: heat must be *added* to start the reaction and *removed* afterwards, so a steam-only system is useless. Two approaches: a two-way (heating and cooling) system, or split duties by preheating reactants and cooling the reactor.
  - **Boiling-liquid coolant** (Fig. 10.9): heat-transfer rate is proportional to coolant temperature, and the boiling point is set by pressure — so the manipulated variable is the **set point of the jacket back-pressure controller**. Easy start-up because heat can be applied externally, efficient cooling because the coefficient is high.
  - **Circulating coolant** (Fig. 10.10): choose **coolant temperature** as the manipulated variable, because it is linear with both heat-transfer rate and reactor temperature; circulate fast for maximum transfer and speed. Without recirculation the secondary dead time varies with coolant flow, which combines with the nonlinear temperature-flow relation (9.11) to produce a **limit cycle even with an equal-percentage valve** — and the cycle is recognisable: a short hot excursion when flow is greatest, a long cold stretch afterward.
  - **Monitoring conversion from heat removal**: condensate flow is a direct indication of heat evolution; with a circulating coolant take flow × ΔT. **Two pitfalls**: when the primary controller calls for more cooling, jacket *inlet* temperature falls before outlet — and the inlet-outlet difference may be only ~5°F, less than a transient in the inlet — so the heat-transfer record looks erratic unless the inlet measurement is **dynamically compensated** (delay it by the jacket's transport delay, e.g. by simulating the jacket with an adjustable length of tubing). Second, reactants and products differ in temperature step, and agitator power adds.
  - **Preheat before mixing**, unless the catalyst is what makes reaction possible; otherwise the reaction can start in the preheater where it cannot be controlled. Once initiated it is usually safe to bypass preheaters.
  - **Regenerative preheat forms a positive feedback loop** (product heats reactant, which changes reaction, which changes product temperature) and can destroy whatever self-regulation the reactor had — preheat temperature must be controlled.
  - **Use the heat of vaporisation** where the reaction runs near a boiling point: reflux a vaporised reactant, or let a vaporised product leave as vapour. This is highly self-regulating but **pressure-sensitive**, and since pressure responds faster than temperature, **control pressure** (throttle the reflux or the vapour leaving the reactor) rather than or along with temperature.
- **Maximising production: the valve-position controller (VPC).** The limit to production is usually heat-transfer capability, and the best indicator of its loading is the **position of the temperature controller's valve**. A VPC on that signal can set feed rate (or temperature set point).
  - The VPC loop carries an **extra lag** not present elsewhere: changing feed rate changes concentration before it changes reaction rate and heat evolution. It works as long as the temperature loop is tight, but needs **all three PID modes**, tuned to the same period as the temperature controller.
  - **Do not control temperature by feed rate alone** with cooling fixed at maximum: there must be a temperature where reaction takes place before feeding begins (raising feed rate at too low a temperature does not raise it, but can build a concentration that overreacts once ignition happens), and feed rate places the **concentration lag and thermal lag in series**. Reactor temperature is too vital to leave to anything but a well-designed heat-transfer system.
  - **Exothermic VPC is the trap**: raising reactor temperature requires *less* cooling, but holding a higher temperature requires more — so a VPC manipulating temperature set point through the cooling valve sees **inverse response**. Simulation showed **only integral action worked**, and its time constant had to be set very long to avoid cycling, making the approach to the new set point very slow. It must also leave enough cooling margin to survive a severe upset — the less stable the reactor, the more margin.
- **Controlling conversion — two conversions, both economic.** **Overall conversion** should approach 100% (unconverted reactant is purged, downgraded or destroyed), but **per-pass conversion** trades against **selectivity**, because serial reactions convert desired product into by-product. Selectivity is usually maximised at **minimum conversion per pass** — achieved by lowering reactant concentration, residence time and temperature — which also minimises catalyst degradation and extends equipment life, but forces recycle. Fisher et al.'s argument: once the capital equipment exists, minimise *operating* cost, which means **maximise recycle rate and hold conversion per pass at the level that just meets production**.
  - To do this you need to manipulate **reactor severity** (temperature) — with recycle at maximum, feed at the production goal and catalyst activity whatever it is, per-pass conversion that fails to match feed rate shows up as **changing inventory of reactant in the recycle stream**, and severity is manipulated to hold that inventory constant. In the hydrocracking example the controlled variable is the **level in the base of the rerun column** (with the HC purge/feed ratio setting overall conversion), and it is a very slow loop — the product passes several vessels first — and non-self-regulating. Recycle rate is set as high as the columns and compressor allow; hydrogen inventory is held by a pressure controller that sets fresh feed to match consumption plus purge.
  - Running at constraints then requires **override protection**: extra level controllers to cut HC feed if conversion cannot be met, and pressure controllers to reduce severity on hydrogen deficiency or relieve gas if compressor capacity is exceeded. These overrides act momentarily while a new steady state is established. **Purge rates are themselves optimised** against the impact of inerts/contaminants and the fate of the purge stream — the hydrocracker's gas purge sets methane concentration in the recycle gas.
- **pH control** — the canonical endpoint problem, hard because the measurement is **logarithmic**.
  - Definitions: pH = −log[H+] (10.25), [OH−] = 10^(pH−14) (10.26), charge balance xA − xB = 10^(−pH) − 10^(pH−14) (10.30). At pH 7 with xA = xB the solution is neutral, and **each pH unit is a decade of concentration difference**.
  - **The neutral range is brutal with strong reagents only**: matching a 10⁻² N acid to ±10⁻⁶ N is 1 part in 10,000 — beyond any valve, pump or flowmeter, so **feedback in a well-mixed vessel with a fast measurement is the only way** to achieve it. Measured sensitivity ≈ **10⁶ pH/N**.
  - **Weak acids and bases are the cure.** With pKa/pKb (10.33), a weak acid's titration curve is flattest where it is half-neutralised, at **pH = pKa** — the buffering point, an inherent self-regulating property. Differentiating gives the process gain dpH/dxB ≈ 0.434/[xA·10^(pKa−pH)] (10.38–10.39): sensitivity depends mainly on **weak-acid concentration and pKa**, not on the reagents. Comparison: a 0.01 N acetic acid solution has a sensitivity of only 0.78 × 10⁴ pH/N, **over 100× easier** than the strong-acid case. Diprotic species buffer at two pH values — carbonates at **6.35 and 10.25**, which is why ground and surface waters tend to regulate their own pH and why neutralisation of carbonate-bearing streams is easier in the 6–7 region.
  - **Titration curve data (Table 10.2)**: xA − xB = 1.0 N at pH 0, 1.0 × 10⁻² at pH 2, 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ at pH 4, 0.99 × 10⁻⁶ at pH 6, 0 at pH 7, −0.99 × 10⁻⁶ at pH 8, −1.0 × 10⁻⁴ at pH 10, −1.0 × 10⁻² at pH 12, −1.0 at pH 14. **Table 10.3** gives pKa/pKb for common species (acetic 4.75, CO2 6.35/10.25, HF 3.17, H2S 7.0/12.9, HClO 7.5, H2SO4 −3/1.99, SO2 1.8/6.8; ammonia 4.75, lime 1.40/2.43, hydrazine 5.5, hydroxylamine 7.97, aluminate 1.6, magnesium hydroxide 2.6, ferric hydroxide 9.3/11.5).
  - **Structure**: a **nonlinear controller** with a widened low-gain zone (the Chap. 5 shape) handles the variable gain; where buffering varies, **adapt the zone width** from the feedforward signals (Fig. 6.20's adaptive scheme). A linear controller is adequate only when the solution is so highly buffered that gain hardly varies.
  - **Do not let a two-reagent system cycle.** Cycling wastes reagent and, worse, there is **no guarantee the final pH after a holding basin equals set point** — one field case ended outside the controlled limit cycle because of an unsymmetrical titration curve plus electrode lag.
  - **Electrode lag** can dominate the loop: precipitate films on the electrode act like a well around a thermocouple, and the lag can stretch the loop period from 2–3 min to **over an hour**. Clean the electrode (mechanical or ultrasonic, or periodic rinse) rather than trying to tune around it.
  - **Rangeability is usually the binding constraint**, not gain. Typical effluent-treatment demands: flow varies 4–5×, the stream may swing from acidic to basic (two reagents), acid/base content varies over decades, and the species type varies from weak to strong so **the pH curve itself changes**. Meanwhile differential meters are accurate to 1% of span only from 15–100% (**6:1**), metering pumps ~**20:1**, control valves **35:1 to 100:1** — yet the requirement can exceed **1000:1**.
    - **Sequence two equal-percentage valves** to get there: two 50:1 equal-percentage valves give a combined turndown approaching **2500:1**. Size the large valve for maximum flow and the small valve so its maximum slightly exceeds the large valve's minimum capability; then plot the large valve's maximum and the small valve's minimum at 100% and 0% controller output, draw the straight line between them, and read the calibration points for the two positioners off that line. The achieved rangeability depends on the limited set of available valve Cv ratios.
    - **Only one valve may be open at a time** — otherwise the overall characteristic is distorted and too much flow arrives at the instant the large valve opens, causing limit cycling in that load range. Use relay logic with an alarm-triggered transfer (about a second) to close the small valve as the large one opens, and to return on decreasing output. If rangeability still falls short, drive the **smallest valve from its own narrow-band proportional controller** — the valve gain is so low that a narrow band is safe and it removes the low-load limit cycle.
    - Where equal-percentage valves are used but a linear overall characteristic is wanted, add a **characterizer** at the controller output (linear overall is the normal goal; the exception is neutralising a *single* weak acid or base whose curve slope tracks reagent demand per 10.39).
  - **Two-reagent sequencing**: acid above 50% controller output, base below, with both closed at 50%. Their **capability is reagent normality × maximum flow** (98% sulfuric acid ≈ 36 N; 10% caustic and lime ≈ 2.8 N); if the two differ, calibrate the positioners to keep loop gain constant — a lime valve delivering twice the equivalent concentration of its acid partner needs twice the operating range, moving the both-closed point to **66.7%**. Sequencing so that the **base** valve opens on loss of signal is preferred (opening acid invites corrosion, and lime's limited solubility caps pH at 12.3), but if both valves must fail closed for air-supply loss, the base-valve positioner must be **reverse-acting** to keep the sequencing right.
  - **Feedforward pH control** (10.40–10.41): mA = 2m − GA(rA − pH), mB = 2(1 − m) − GB(pH − rB), with m the feedback controller output, rA/rB the influent pH values at which full reagent delivery occurs at 50% output, and G and r as manual tuning adjustments (proportional band and set point of the feedforward controllers, proportional mode only). An **equal-percentage valve provides the logarithmic feedforward characterisation**, but it makes feedback gain vary — hence the adaptive low-gain zone width driven by the feedforward signals, which must not close a loop around the pH controller.
  - **And then the honest verdict**: adjusting feedforward pH control is difficult and frustrating; when ionic species change, the influent-pH-to-reagent-demand relationship changes markedly, and **a change in influent pH can drive the reagent valve the wrong way** (0.1 N HCl at pH 1.0 versus 1.0 N acetic acid buffered with sodium acetate at pH 4.75 — adding the mixture *raises* pH while the base demand grows tenfold). Feedforward cannot be counted on for the load-response improvement it delivers elsewhere; **feedback in a well-mixed vessel with a carefully designed, low-dead-time reagent-delivery system is the better answer**.

## Key Concepts
- **Equilibrium constant K = kf/kr** (10.4): the ratio of forward to reverse rate coefficients, temperature- and catalyst-dependent.
- **Conversion** y, and the **conversion per pass** versus **overall conversion** distinction that governs selectivity and recycle.
- **Thermal steady-state gain Kt** (10.20) and **thermal time constant τt = KtVρC/UA** (10.21). Sign of Kt = the entire control story: positive = self-regulating, infinite = non-self-regulating, negative = unstable.
- **Negative resistance** (Chap. 5, Eq. 5.12) as the phase behaviour of an unstable reactor.
- **Inversion of the design equation**: since Kt and τt depend on F, x0 and UA, controllability is a **design variable**, not a tuning skill.
- **Severity**: reactor temperature used as the handle on per-pass conversion, held against a recycle inventory.
- **Valve-position controller (VPC)**: a controller whose measurement is another controller's output, used to load a constrained resource (heat transfer) to its limit.
- **Endpoint control**: feedback on a final property (pH, conductivity) rather than on a ratio — essential where the process is non-self-regulating or where ratio accuracy is unobtainable.
- **Buffering point / pH = pKa**: the half-neutralised condition where the titration curve is flattest.
- **Rangeability versus gain**: in pH systems the limiting specification is usually the ratio of maximum to minimum reagent flow, not loop gain.

## Mental Models
- **A reactor's control difficulty is designed in.** Before tuning, compute Kt and τd/τt. If τd/τt < −0.5, no controller will save it — change F, x0 or UA.
- **Heat removal is the production limit.** Valve position on the temperature controller is the honest measure of how loaded the exchanger is.
- **Selectivity wants low conversion per pass; economics wants maximum recycle.** Severity is the knob that reconciles them.
- **Purge is a control loop.** Inerts and poisons accumulate by definition; the purge rate is an optimised variable, not a bleed.
- **For pH, ask what the curve is doing before asking what the controller is doing.** Sensitivity spans six decades between strong and weak reagents.
- **Reagent capability = normality × maximum flow.** Sequence valves on capability, not on valve size.
- **Feedforward can be pointed the wrong way** when the chemistry changes underneath it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tuning an exothermic reactor wider to fix cycling** — widening the band raises the second resonant peak (near 9τd) while lowering the first; you move the oscillation, you do not remove it.
- **Using an integral controller on a recycled-solvent composition loop** — the process is non-self-regulating (10.24), so it will wind up; use endpoint feedback with proportional/PD action.
- **Controlling reactor temperature by manipulating feed rate with cooling fixed at maximum** — concentration and thermal lags in series, and no ignition temperature established before feeding.
- **A VPC driving temperature set point on an exothermic reactor with only PI** — it faces inverse response; simulation shows only a very slow integral mode survives.
- **Adding heat in a preheater and removing it in the reactor** — thermodynamically silly and it invites reaction where you cannot control it.
- **Uncontrolled regenerative preheat** — closes a positive feedback loop that can destroy the reactor's self-regulation.
- **Ignoring agitator power and reactant/product temperature differences** when inferring heat evolution from coolant flow × ΔT.
- **Tuning around electrode lag** instead of cleaning the electrode.
- **Opening both sequenced reagent valves at once**, or sequencing on valve size instead of normality × flow.
- **Trusting feedforward alone on a mixed strong/weak waste stream.**

## Worked Example
**Back-mixed reactor, 50% conversion, T = 230°F, V/F = 1 h.** Because kV/F = 1 is exactly the point of maximum slope for a back-mixed reactor, this is the *most* sensitive operating point available: the steady-state slope is 1/95°F and the **dynamic** slope is dy/dT = (E/RT²)y = 1/95°F × ... — computed from 10.15 with y = 0.5, this dynamic slope is what enters the stability denominator.

Now evaluate Kt = −UA/[UA + FρC − HrFx0(dy/dT)x] (10.20). Feed or concentration reduction reduces the HrFx0(dy/dT)x term, so **Kt moves from negative toward positive** — the reactor becomes self-regulating. That is the design lever, quantified. With τt = KtVρC/UA (10.21), a negative Kt makes τt negative, and the stability limits become:

- Steady state: **P < −100Kt** — the band cannot be wider than the process gain.
- Dynamic: **P > 100Kt·τd/τt** (10.23) — and if the loop's dead time (say 1 min from a circulation or sample path) is comparable to |τt|, the two limits cross and there is **no band that satisfies both**.

Read the numbers off Table 10.1 for a 1-min dead time and a thermal time constant of, say, 10 min (τd/τt = −0.1): I = 1.70τd = **1.7 min**, D = 0.60τd = **0.6 min**, and P ≈ 11% of −Kt — a workable band. Push the reaction rate up until τt falls to 1 min (τd/τt = −1.0) and the table says **no stability margin**, which is the chapter's point restated as arithmetic: the same physical reactor, with 5 min more of coolant flow dead time or 10× the reaction rate, goes from easily controllable to impossible without any change to the controller.
